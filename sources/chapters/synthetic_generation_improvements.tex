\section{Introduction}

While the previous chapter demonstrated that our synthetic generation approach produces high-quality documents that can match or exceed the performance of models trained on real data across multiple benchmarks, a critical question remains: \textit{do these synthetic documents truly preserve patient privacy, or do they inadvertently leak identifying information?} This question is paramount in medical contexts, where even seemingly anonymized data can pose re-identification risks that violate patient confidentiality and regulatory requirements.

Our generation pipeline was designed with privacy preservation as a core principle—using only UMLS concept keywords rather than raw text, and ensuring that only numerical scores cross the privacy boundary between private and public environments. However, architectural safeguards alone do not guarantee privacy. The primary risk lies in re-identification: an adversary with access to the published synthetic dataset may attempt to link synthetic documents back to specific patients whose data informed their creation. Even without explicit personally identifiable information, the selection, ordering, and combination of medical concepts may retain patient-specific patterns that enable such attacks.

This chapter conducts a rigorous privacy analysis of our synthetic document generation pipeline and proposes mitigation strategies to address identified vulnerabilities. Our contributions are threefold:

\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Privacy vulnerability assessment:} We implement two complementary attack scenarios—linkage attacks (1-vs-N) and discrimination attacks (1-vs-2)—using both lexical (TF-IDF) and semantic similarity metrics to quantify re-identification risks across MIMIC-III and AlpaCare datasets.
    
    \item \textbf{Post-processing mitigation strategies:} We propose and evaluate two privacy-enhancing post-processing methods: standard rephrasing and keyword perturbation. Our results demonstrate that keyword perturbation reduces linkage attack success rates by 50-60 percentage points, achieving 57-63 pp reduction compared to only 2-24 pp for standard rephrasing.
    
    \item \textbf{Privacy-utility trade-off analysis:} We measure the impact of privacy mitigations on downstream task performance, demonstrating that strong privacy guarantees can be achieved while preserving data utility for clinical NLP applications.
\end{enumerate}

The findings reveal that while baseline synthetic documents exhibit concerning re-identification vulnerabilities—with discrimination attacks achieving 98-99\% accuracy—targeted keyword perturbation effectively disrupts patient-specific fingerprints. Our mitigation strategies enable the production of high-quality synthetic datasets with significantly reduced re-identification risks. These datasets may be published or shared according to the security requirements defined by the data provider, offering a practical pathway for privacy-conscious medical data dissemination.

The remainder of this chapter is organized as follows. Section~\ref{sec:privacy_related} reviews related work on privacy attacks and text rewriting for privacy preservation. \section{Related Work}
\label{sec:privacy_related}

This section reviews existing work on privacy attacks against synthetic data and text rewriting methods for privacy preservation, positioning our contributions within this landscape.

\paragraph{Privacy Attacks on Synthetic Data}

The privacy of synthetic data has been extensively studied through various attack paradigms. Membership inference attacks attempt to determine whether a specific record was part of the training data used to generate synthetic samples~\cite{shokri2017membership,carlini2022membership,mattern2023membership}. These attacks exploit models' tendency to overfit their training data and therefore exhibit lower loss values for training members. Recent work has demonstrated that neighbourhood-based attacks, which compare model scores for a given sample to scores of synthetically generated neighbour texts, can outperform traditional reference-based approaches~\cite{mattern2023membership}.

Linkage attacks, which are central to our evaluation, attempt to link synthetic records back to their source individuals by exploiting similarities between synthetic and real data~\cite{narayanan2008robust}. In the medical domain, re-identification risks are particularly concerning due to the sensitive nature of health information. Prior work has demonstrated that clinical notes, even after de-identification, may retain sufficient information for re-identification through quasi-identifiers or writing style patterns~\cite{bencheikhlarbi2023clinical}. Recent studies have shown that de-identification alone does not fully protect patient privacy, as membership inference attacks can still succeed even on de-identified data~\cite{sarkar2024deidentification}.

\paragraph{Text Rewriting for Privacy Preservation}

Differential privacy (DP) has emerged as a principled framework for privacy-preserving text generation. DP-Rewrite~\cite{igamberdiev2022dprewrite} proposed reproducible methods for differentially private text rewriting, while DP-BART~\cite{igamberdiev2023dpbart} introduced a BART-based approach operating under local differential privacy guarantees.

Most relevant to our work, Igamberdiev et al.~\cite{igamberdiev2024rewriteagain} proposed "Just Rewrite It Again," a post-processing method that rewrites differentially private text a second time to improve both semantic similarity and empirical privacy protection. Their key insight is that aligning rewritten texts with their original counterparts through an additional rewriting pass can simultaneously enhance utility and privacy. We build upon this insight but diverge in a critical aspect: rather than applying generic paraphrasing, we explicitly target the medical keywords extracted during generation, replacing them with semantically equivalent synonyms to disrupt patient-specific lexical fingerprints.

\paragraph{Positioning of Our Approach}

Our approach builds upon these existing methods and combines several strengths:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Domain-specific targeting:} We leverage the medical ontology (UMLS) used during generation to identify and perturb the specific terms most likely to enable re-identification, rather than applying generic text rewriting.
    
    \item \textbf{Post-processing flexibility:} Our mitigation strategies operate as post-processing steps, making them applicable to any synthetic generation pipeline without requiring architectural modifications or retraining.
    
    \item \textbf{Empirical attack evaluation:} We evaluate privacy through realistic attack scenarios (linkage and discrimination) using both lexical and semantic similarity metrics, providing practical insights into re-identification risks.
    
    \item \textbf{Privacy-utility trade-off analysis:} We systematically measure the impact of privacy mitigations on downstream clinical NLP tasks, enabling informed decisions based on the security requirements of the data provider.
\end{itemize}

\section{Threat Model}
\label{sec:threat_model}

This section formalizes our threat model and describes the attack methodologies used to evaluate re-identification risks in synthetic clinical documents.
We consider an adversary with the following characteristics and capabilities:

\paragraph{Adversary's access.} The adversary has access to the published synthetic dataset but cannot directly query the generative model. This reflects realistic deployment scenarios where synthetic data is released for research purposes while the generation infrastructure remains private within the hospital environment.

\paragraph{Adversary's goal.} The adversary attempts to link synthetic documents back to specific patients whose data informed their creation. A successful attack constitutes a privacy breach, as it reveals that a particular patient's medical history contributed to the synthetic dataset.

\paragraph{Adversary's knowledge.} We assume the adversary possesses some real patient documents (e.g., from a data breach, insider access, or overlapping datasets) and attempts to find corresponding synthetic documents. This represents a realistic threat in healthcare settings where partial data exposure may occur.

\section{Attack Methodology}
\paragraph{Distinction from canary attacks.} Canary attacks~\cite{carlini2019secret_sharer} evaluate memorization by inserting unique identifiable sequences (canaries) into training data, then testing whether the trained model can reproduce these sequences when prompted. This approach is inapplicable to our context for several reasons:

\begin{itemize}
    \item End users only access the published synthetic dataset without the ability to query the generative model directly, precluding completion-based extraction attacks.
    \item Our generation method relies on UMLS keywords that are inherently non-identifying—they contain no personal information such as names or identifiers that could be memorized and later extracted.
    \item The model generates new text guided by these keywords rather than performing direct completion of patient documents, reducing the risk of verbatim memorization.
\end{itemize}

\paragraph{Attack selection} Following recent work on membership inference attacks~\cite{mattern2023membership}, we evaluate privacy through two complementary attack scenarios of varying strength. The linkage attack (1-vs-N) represents a realistic but challenging scenario where the adversary must identify the source patient among all candidates. The discrimination attack (1-vs-2) represents a theoretically stronger attack where the adversary only needs to distinguish between two candidates—this scenario assumes the adversary has prior knowledge narrowing down the search space, making it a more demanding privacy test. Success at the discrimination attack indicates that synthetic documents retain patient-specific fingerprints even when the global attribution task is difficult.

\subsection{Linkage Attack (1-vs-N)}
\label{subsec:linkage_attack}

The linkage attack simulates an adversary attempting to globally attribute each synthetic document to its source patient among the entire dataset.

\paragraph{Attack procedure} For each synthetic document $s_i$, we compute similarity scores against all real patient documents $\{r_1, r_2, ..., r_N\}$ in the private dataset. The predicted source patient is the one whose document achieves the highest similarity score:

\begin{equation}
    \hat{p}_i = \arg\max_{j \in \{1,...,N\}} \text{sim}(s_i, r_j)
\end{equation}

\paragraph{Success metric.} The attack succeeds if the predicted patient $\hat{p}_i$ matches the true source patient $p_i$. We report the overall success rate across all synthetic documents:

\begin{equation}
    \text{Linkage Success Rate} = \frac{1}{M} \sum_{i=1}^{M} \mathbb{1}[\hat{p}_i = p_i]
\end{equation}

where $M$ is the number of synthetic documents.

\paragraph{Random baseline.} The random baseline for this attack is approximately $\frac{1}{N}$, which is near-zero for large datasets. Any success rate substantially above this threshold indicates privacy leakage.

\subsection{Discrimination Attack (1-vs-2)}
\label{subsec:discrimination_attack}

The discrimination attack evaluates whether an adversary can distinguish between two candidate patients when presented with a synthetic document known to derive from one of them.

\paragraph{Attack procedure.} For each synthetic document $s_i$ generated from patient $p_i$, we randomly select a different patient $p_j$ ($j \neq i$). The adversary must identify which of the two candidates is the true source based on similarity scores:

\begin{equation}
    \hat{p} = \arg\max_{p \in \{p_i, p_j\}} \text{sim}(s_i, r_p)
\end{equation}

\paragraph{Success metric.} The attack succeeds if the adversary correctly identifies the true source patient. We repeat this test multiple times per synthetic document with different random candidates and report the average success rate.

\paragraph{Random baseline.} The theoretical baseline is 50\% (random guessing). Performance significantly above this threshold indicates that synthetic documents retain patient-specific characteristics enabling discrimination.

\paragraph{Realistic threat scenario.} The discrimination attack represents a particularly realistic scenario where an adversary has narrowed down potential patients to a small set (e.g., patients treated during a specific time period or for a specific condition) and must identify the correct individual.

\subsection{Similarity Metrics}
\label{subsec:similarity_metrics}

We evaluate attacks using two complementary similarity approaches to capture different types of information leakage:

\paragraph{Lexical similarity (TF-IDF).} We compute Term Frequency-Inverse Document Frequency vectors for each document and measure cosine similarity between synthetic and real documents:

\begin{equation}
    \text{sim}_{\text{TF-IDF}}(s, r) = \frac{\text{TF-IDF}(s) \cdot \text{TF-IDF}(r)}{||\text{TF-IDF}(s)|| \cdot ||\text{TF-IDF}(r)||}
\end{equation}

We use a vocabulary of up to 5,000 features. TF-IDF captures lexical overlap and is particularly sensitive to rare medical terms that may serve as patient fingerprints.

\paragraph{Semantic similarity} We encode documents using a pre-trained sentence transformer (all-mpnet-base-v2) and compute cosine similarity in the embedding space:

\begin{equation}
    \text{sim}_{\text{semantic}}(s, r) = \frac{E(s) \cdot E(r)}{||E(s)|| \cdot ||E(r)||}
\end{equation}

where $E(\cdot)$ denotes the embedding function. Semantic similarity captures meaning-level correspondence and may detect re-identification risks even when exact lexical matches are absent.

\paragraph{Complementary evaluation.} These two metrics capture different vulnerability profiles. TF-IDF is sensitive to keyword-level leakage, while semantic embeddings may reveal deeper structural or conceptual similarities. Evaluating both provides a comprehensive view of privacy risks and helps assess the effectiveness of different mitigation strategies.

\section{Privacy Vulnerabilities in Baseline Generation}
\label{sec:baseline_vulnerabilities}

Before evaluating mitigation strategies, we first assess the privacy vulnerabilities present in synthetic documents generated by our baseline pipeline (described in Chapter~\ref{chap:synthetic}) without any post-processing privacy enhancements.

\subsection{Experimental Setup}

We evaluate re-identification risks on synthetic documents generated at step 2 (DPO-2) of our reward-based generation pipeline, using both seed ratios (4\% and 6\%) for MIMIC-III, and DPO-1 and DP-SFT configurations for AlpaCare.

\subsubsection{Datasets and Patient Identification}

Each dataset requires specific preprocessing to establish patient identifiers suitable for re-identification evaluation.

\paragraph{MIMIC-III} We use the History of Present Illness sections extracted from discharge summaries. Each document is naturally associated with a unique patient admission. To ensure meaningful evaluation, we filtered the dataset to retain only patients with more than 5 documents in the evaluation subset, ensuring sufficient material for robust similarity computation. After filtering, our evaluation dataset comprises 1,764 documents for the 4\% configuration and 1,819 documents for the 6\% configuration.

\paragraph{AlpaCare} The AlpaCare dataset presents a methodological challenge: conversational medical messages are not naturally attributed to unique patients. To enable meaningful privacy evaluation, we created pseudo-patient identifiers through clustering. We applied k-means clustering (k=4000) on 10,000 source response texts using BioLORD-2023-M~\cite{remy2024biolord} sentence embeddings via FAISS, then filtered out clusters containing fewer than 3 samples. Since each synthetic document generated in Chapter~\ref{chap:synthetic} is derived from keywords extracted from a specific source response, we propagate the cluster ID from the source to its corresponding synthetic document. This mapping enables re-identification evaluation by linking synthetic documents to their pseudo-patient origins. Our evaluation includes 3,698 documents for the DPO-1 configuration and 6,062 documents for the DP-SFT configuration. \subsection{Analysis of Information Leakage Sources}

The results reveal an important finding regarding the source of privacy leakage in our pipeline. Comparing DP-SFT and DPO configurations on AlpaCare highlights a critical pattern:

\paragraph{DP-SFT exhibits lower re-identification risk.} The DP-SFT configuration, where the model is trained only with an initial supervised fine-tuning phase on a small seed dataset, achieves linkage attack success rates of 24.78\% (TF-IDF) and 26.31\% (embeddings). This relatively lower vulnerability suggests that the base generation process, guided solely by keyword prompts, does not inherently produce highly identifiable documents. Discrimination attacks on DP-SFT also show reduced effectiveness (69.65\% TF-IDF, 79.00\% embeddings) compared to other configurations.

\paragraph{DPO reinforcement amplifies leakage.} In contrast, the DPO-1 configuration exhibits substantially higher linkage attack success rates of 77.88\% (TF-IDF) and 81.99\% (embeddings). The key difference is the preference optimization step, which trains the model to generate documents that score higher similarity to real patient documents. This reinforcement mechanism, while improving document quality (as demonstrated in Chapter~\ref{chap:synthetic}), inadvertently teaches the model to reproduce patient-specific patterns. Notably, DPO-1 shows higher semantic vulnerability (81.99\%) than lexical vulnerability (77.88\%), suggesting that preference optimization reinforces deep semantic patterns beyond surface-level terminology.

\paragraph{Implications for reward-based generation.} This observation reveals a fundamental tension in our approach: the reward signal based on similarity to real documents is effective at improving synthetic document quality, but it simultaneously reinforces patterns that enable re-identification. The model learns features that are both clinically relevant and patient-identifying, as these characteristics are correlated in real medical documents.

This finding also demonstrates that even a simple similarity-based reinforcement mechanism can lead the model to capture potentially sensitive patient information. The evaluator model does not explicitly encode patient identities, yet the optimization process discovers and exploits patient-specific fingerprints to maximize reward scores.

\paragraph{Sources of leakage.} We hypothesize that the reinforced patterns include:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Keyword sequences:} The ordered sequence of UMLS concepts extracted from a patient's document creates a distinctive fingerprint, even though individual concepts are non-identifying.
    
    \item \textbf{Concept co-occurrence patterns:} Specific combinations of diagnoses, treatments, and procedures may be rare enough to enable re-identification.
    
    \item \textbf{Narrative structure:} The temporal ordering and narrative flow of medical events may retain patient-specific characteristics that the model learns to reproduce during preference optimization.
\end{itemize}

These vulnerabilities, particularly those amplified by the reward-based training process, motivate the development of post-processing mitigation strategies presented in the following section.

\section{Mitigation Strategies}
\label{sec:mitigation}

Given the privacy vulnerabilities identified in baseline synthetic documents, we propose and evaluate two post-processing mitigation strategies inspired by recent work on text rewriting for privacy preservation~\cite{meisenbacherJustRewriteIt2024}. Both methods operate on already-generated synthetic documents, making them applicable to any generation pipeline without requiring retraining.

\subsection{Common Implementation Setup}
\label{subsec:mitigation_setup}

Both mitigation strategies leverage a large language model to rewrite synthetic documents. We use Llama-3.3-70B-Instruct with a temperature of 0.7 and a maximum output length of 512 tokens. The two approaches differ only in their prompting strategy, as illustrated in Figure~\ref{fig:mitigation_prompts}: standard rephrasing applies generic paraphrasing instructions, while keyword perturbation explicitly provides the list of extracted keywords that must be replaced.

The model autonomously selects appropriate replacements while preserving clinical accuracy. This targeted approach aims to disrupt the distinctive keyword sequences that enable re-identification.

The results demonstrate dramatic privacy improvements for TF-IDF linkage attacks across all datasets, with reductions ranging from 19.50 to 63.47 percentage points. Notably, MIMIC-III benefits most from this approach: while standard rephrasing achieved only 2-5 pp reduction, keyword perturbation achieves 57-59 pp reduction. Keyword perturbation shows substantial effectiveness against semantic linkage attacks, with reductions of 12-38 pp across all configurations. TF-IDF-based discrimination achieves 86-95\% success rates on MIMIC-III and AlpaCare DPO-1 (reductions of 5-12 pp from baseline), while semantic discrimination remains virtually unchanged at 97-99\% (reductions of less than 2 pp). The only exception is AlpaCare DP-SFT, which shows substantially lower discrimination rates (57.21\% TF-IDF, 79.68\% semantic), confirming that DP-SFT training produces intrinsically more private synthetic documents.

\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:privacy_discussion}

This section synthesizes our findings, discusses their implications for practical deployment, and acknowledges the limitations of our approach.

\paragraph{Key Findings}

Our privacy analysis reveals several important insights about synthetic medical document generation:

The primary vector of privacy leakage in clinical documents is medical terminology. The dramatic effectiveness of keyword perturbation (19-63 pp reduction in TF-IDF linkage attacks, 12-38 pp for semantic) compared to standard rephrasing (2-24 pp TF-IDF, 3-12 pp semantic) demonstrates that patient-specific fingerprints are primarily encoded in the selection and combination of medical concepts rather than in general linguistic patterns.

The reward-based training process amplifies re-identification risks. The comparison between DP-SFT (24.78\% TF-IDF, 26.31\% semantic linkage success) and DPO (77.88\% TF-IDF, 81.99\% semantic linkage success) configurations on AlpaCare reveals that preference optimization, while improving document quality, teaches the model to reproduce patient-specific patterns. This finding highlights a fundamental tension in similarity-based reward mechanisms: optimizing for resemblance to real documents inadvertently reinforces identifying characteristics.

Targeted keyword perturbation achieves 19-63 pp reduction in TF-IDF linkage attacks and 12-38 pp in semantic linkage attacks, compared to only 2-24 pp and 3-12 pp respectively for generic rephrasing, for comparable utility costs. This substantial difference underscores the importance of domain-specific privacy interventions in medical NLP, where standardized terminology creates distinctive patterns that generic text rewriting fails to address. However, discrimination attacks remain largely effective (57-95\% TF-IDF, 79-99\% semantic after keyword perturbation), indicating that lexical disruption alone does not eliminate patient-specific structure.

\paragraph{Implications for Deployment}

Our findings have concrete implications for the deployment of synthetic medical datasets:

Synthetic datasets can be published with appropriate mitigations. Applying keyword perturbation reduces linkage attack success rates to 5.28-30.68\%, substantially lowering re-identification risks. These mitigated datasets may be shared according to the security requirements defined by the data provider, enabling broader access to medical NLP training data while respecting patient privacy.

The generative model itself should not be publicly deployed. While synthetic datasets can be released after mitigation, the underlying generative model poses greater risks. Direct access to the model would enable adversaries to generate targeted queries, potentially extracting more patient-specific information than what is present in a fixed synthetic dataset.

Security requirements should be adapted to the deployment context. The acceptable level of residual re-identification risk depends on the sensitivity of the source data, the potential adversary capabilities, and the regulatory framework. Data providers should assess these factors when determining which mitigation level to apply and whether additional safeguards (e.g., data use agreements, access controls) are necessary.

\paragraph{Connection with Differential Privacy} Our empirical mitigation strategies are complementary to formal differential privacy (DP) guarantees, not substitutes. Differential privacy provides mathematical bounds on information leakage, while our approach reduces empirical re-identification success without formal guarantees.

A promising direction for future work is combining both approaches: using DP during model training to bound worst-case leakage, while applying keyword perturbation as post-processing to further reduce practical re-identification risks. This layered defense strategy could provide both theoretical guarantees and strong empirical protection.

\section{Limitations}

Several limitations should be considered when interpreting our results.

\paragraph{Discrimination attacks remain effective.} While our mitigations significantly reduce linkage attack success rates (from 25-90\% to 5-31\% for TF-IDF, from 26-82\% to 14-44\% for semantic), discrimination attacks remain highly effective even after keyword perturbation (57-95\% TF-IDF, 79-99\% semantic). This indicates that although global re-identification becomes difficult, patient-specific semantic patterns persist in the synthetic documents. When an adversary can narrow down candidates to a small set, distinguishing between them remains feasible. This represents a fundamental limitation: our approach disrupts lexical fingerprints but does not fully eliminate the underlying patient-specific structure.

\paragraph{Robustness to adaptive attacks is untested.} Our evaluation assumes an adversary using standard similarity metrics (TF-IDF, cosine similarity). A sophisticated adversary aware of our mitigation strategy could potentially develop adaptive attacks, such as training a model to reverse keyword perturbations by learning common synonym substitution patterns, or exploiting semantic embeddings that capture meaning beyond lexical overlap. Evaluating robustness against such adaptive adversaries remains an important direction for future work.

\paragraph{Utility evaluation on MIMIC-III is preliminary.} The downstream task evaluation for privacy-mitigated documents was conducted with a single run rather than multiple seeds, limiting our ability to assess variance. While the consistency of trends across configurations suggests reliable observations, more comprehensive evaluation would strengthen these conclusions.

\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:privacy_conclusion}

This chapter presented a comprehensive privacy analysis of synthetic clinical document generation and proposed effective mitigation strategies to reduce re-identification risks.

Our evaluation revealed that baseline synthetic documents, despite being generated from non-identifying UMLS keywords, retain patient-specific fingerprints that enable linkage attacks with 25-90\% success rates. We identified that the reward-based training process amplifies these vulnerabilities: preference optimization, while improving document quality, teaches the model to reproduce patterns that facilitate re-identification.

To address these vulnerabilities, we proposed and evaluated two post-processing mitigation strategies. Keyword perturbation, which explicitly targets and replaces medical terminology with synonyms, achieves dramatic privacy improvements, reducing linkage attack success by 50-60 percentage points across all evaluated datasets. This approach consistently outperforms standard rephrasing (57-63 pp reduction vs 2-24 pp), demonstrating that domain-specific interventions are essential for privacy preservation in medical NLP.

The privacy-utility trade-off analysis shows that keyword perturbation can be applied with acceptable utility costs. On conversational medical tasks (AlpaCare), utility degradation is negligible ($<$0.03 average preference). On terminology-sensitive tasks (MIMIC-III ICD classification), the trade-off is more pronounced but remains manageable for simpler classification tasks.

Our contributions enable the production of high-quality synthetic medical datasets with significantly reduced re-identification risks. These datasets may be published or shared according to the security requirements defined by the data provider, offering a practical pathway for privacy-conscious medical data dissemination. The best configuration identified—DP-SFT generation combined with keyword perturbation—achieves a linkage attack success rate of only 5.28\%, providing strong privacy guarantees suitable for broader data sharing.

Future work should address remaining challenges, including discrimination attacks which remain effective even after mitigation, robustness to adaptive adversaries, and the combination of our empirical mitigations with formal differential privacy guarantees.
