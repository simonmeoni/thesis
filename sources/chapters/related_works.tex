\section{Resources}
\label{sec:resources}
Biomedical and clinical Natural Language Processing (NLP) has advanced rapidly in recent years, driven in large part by the availability of large-scale datasets. Foundational resources such as MIMIC-III \citep{johnsonMIMICIIIFreelyAccessible2016}, MIMIC-IV, and PhysioNet \citep{goldbergerPhysioBankPhysioToolkitPhysioNet2000} provide de-identified clinical data that have enabled progress across a variety of tasks, from phenotyping to temporal event extraction. Shared tasks like the i2b2/n2c2 challenges \citep{uzunerPracticalApplicationsNatural2015} have further spurred development by releasing annotated datasets for de-identification, entity and relation extraction, and clinical concept extraction.

 Several initiatives have emerged to broaden clinical NLP beyond the English-centric paradigm. For example, FRASIMED provides the largest automatically annotated French corpus of clinical cases by leveraging cross-lingual projection from English and Spanish resources \citep{zaghir2024frasimed}, while ClinText-SP has released the most extensive Spanish clinical corpus to date, together with the domain-specific encoder RigoBERTa \citep{subiesClinTextSPRigoBERTaClinical2025}. Multilingual efforts have also been proposed at the European level, such as the E3C-3.0 corpus, which offers both native and projected clinical case reports across more than ten languages, including French, Italian, Spanish, Basque, and Polish \citep{ghosh2025e3c}. At an even larger scale, MMedC and its associated benchmark MMedBench \citep{qiu2024building} provide billions of multilingual tokens across six languages, aiming to support the training and evaluation of foundation models in medicine. In parallel, Medical mT5 has been introduced as a multilingual text-to-text model trained on a large-scale medical corpus spanning English, Spanish, French, and Italian, accompanied by evaluation benchmarks for multilingual argument mining and question answering \citep{garciaferrero2024medicalmt5}. Despite these advances, the overall landscape remains strongly dominated by English: the majority of publicly available corpora are either English-only or translated from English sources, and high-resource settings outside English are rare. As a result, the development of robust multilingual clinical NLP systems is still constrained by limited coverage, heterogeneous annotation practices, and the scarcity of native, de-identified clinical text in most languages. While these initiatives broaden linguistic coverage, they remain fragmented, largely European, and heavily reliant on annotation projection, raising concerns about linguistic bias and clinical validity in under-resourced settings \citep{villena2025nlp}.

Beyond the foundational corpora and multilingual initiatives, a new generation of resources has emerged to address gaps that traditional information extraction benchmarks fail to cover. These corpora explicitly target higher-level clinical reasoning, temporal modelling, data scarcity in under-resourced languages, and privacy constraints, reflecting a shift from information retrieval tasks toward more complex and deployment-oriented challenges. ReasonMed \citep{sunReasonMed370KMultiAgent2025} introduces 370K multi-agent generated cases annotated with chain-of-thought and error refinement steps, aimed at advancing medical reasoning in large language models (LLMs). Temporal modelling is supported by resources such as PMOA-TTS (PubMed Open Access Textual Time Series) \citep{noroozizadehPMOATTSIntroducingPubMed2025}, comprising 124,699 case reports converted into rich event–time graphs. Multilingual and under-resourced settings have benefited from releases like ClinText-SP, the largest clinical corpus in Spanish, accompanied by the RigoBERTa encoder \citep{subiesClinTextSPRigoBERTaClinical2025}.

Other synthetic or semi-synthetic datasets address privacy barriers, including Synthetic4Health, which generates de-identified clinical letters evaluated on downstream NER tasks \citep{smithSynthetic4Health2025}, and Biomed-Enriched, which leverages LLaMA-3.1-70B to annotate over 400K PubMed paragraphs with biomedical entities and reasoning cues, resulting in measurable gains on domain-specific benchmarks \citep{touchentBiomedEnrichedBiomedicalDataset2025}. Domain-specific resources also continue to expand into specialized niches, such as TFDR, a curated dataset capturing traditional formula disease relationships from complementary medicine literature \citep{liuTFDRDataset2025}. 

MIMIC-IV has become a cornerstone resource in clinical NLP. Its modular architecture \citep{johnson2023mimiciv_SciData} makes it easy to curate highly focused, task-specific subsets. For example, the “Medication Extraction Labels for MIMIC-IV-Note” offers annotations for 600 discharge summaries that enable precise medication entity extraction \citep{goel2023medicationLabels}. Similarly, the DiReCT dataset consists of 511 SOAP-format clinical notes annotated with diagnostic reasoning trees, offering a valuable benchmark for reasoning evaluation \citep{wang2024direct}. The MIMIC-IV-BHC subset pairs clinical notes with concise Brief Hospital Course summaries—ideal for benchmarking LLM summarization in clinical settings \citep{aali2024mimic_bhc}. These subsets demonstrate that MIMIC-IV isn’t merely a large dataset: it’s a versatile ecosystem enabling fine-grained clinical NLP tasks across extraction, reasoning, and summarization domains.

Beyond datasets, aggregation and benchmarking efforts have scaled up. Longitudinal datasets such as EHRSHOT \citep{wornowEHRSHOTEHRBenchmark2023} and large-scale benchmarks like DRAGON \citep{bosmaDRAGONBenchmarkClinical2025} provide new opportunities for evaluating both traditional NLP pipelines and generative foundation models in clinical contexts. BigBIO \citep{friesBigBIOFrameworkDataCentric2022} has unified over a hundred biomedical datasets into a standardised framework, facilitating multi-task and cross-dataset evaluation. Recent shared tasks at BioNLP 2025 further diversify evaluation scenarios, covering synthetic model auditing (SMAFIRA~\citep{neves-etal-2025-smafira}), clinical question answering from EHRs (ArchEHR-QA~\citep{soni-demnerfushman-2025-archehrqa}), radiology report simplification (BioLaySumm~\citep{xiao-etal-2025-biolaysumm}), and LLM-based deception detection (ClinIQLink~\citep{colelough-etal-2025-cliniqlink}), reflecting a shift toward factuality, explainability, and robust cross-domain performance assessment. While aggregation efforts promise standardisation, they risk oversimplifying diverse clinical documentation practices, and shared tasks may privilege leaderboard optimisation over deployment-oriented evaluation. Moreover, these initiatives rarely capture the hospital-centric constraints of real-world deployments, where variability in workflows, institutional policies, and clinical priorities critically shape the performance and adoption of NLP systems.

However, substantial evidence indicates that even widely used datasets like MIMIC-III and MIMIC-IV do not guarantee real-world performance. Issues such as limited institutional diversity, inconsistent documentation styles, and heterogeneous coding schemes undermine the generalizability of models trained on these corpora \citep{elangovanPrinciplesClinicalResearch2024}. In a multi-site study, \citet{adekkanattuEvaluatingPortabilityNLP2019} found that an NLP system for extracting echocardiogram concepts exhibited substantial performance degradation when deployed outside its development environment, with accuracy varying significantly across hospitals like Weill Cornell Medicine, Mayo Clinic, and Northwestern. These findings echo broader concerns in clinical NLP: in-domain test set performance often overestimates a model’s actual utility in deployment, and few studies rigorously assess external validity \citep{frailenavarroClinicalNamedEntity2023}. Such discrepancies highlight a persistent gap between public benchmark performance and the operational demands of hospital-based NLP systems, where documentation practices, terminology, and note structure vary drastically across institutions, and where robust generalisation remains an open challenge despite the growing variety of available resources. Moreover, demographic biases—such as skewed age, gender, or ethnicity distributions in widely used datasets—further limit portability, as models trained on these populations may fail to generalize to more diverse patient cohorts \cite{roosli2022fairness,pmlr-v106-nestor19a}.

\section{Data Augmentation}
\subsection{Pre-LLM}
Data scarcity continues to constrain the performance of modern clinical NLP systems, especially as clinical text diverges in style, structure, and vocabulary across institutions. Data augmentation—creating additional training examples from existing data—has gained traction as a pragmatic solution across several NLP domains. However, the discrete and high-stakes nature of clinical language complicates naive application of off-the-shelf augmentation methods.

A range of classical text augmentation strategies have been widely adopted in NLP, forming the foundation for many subsequent developments. One well-known approach is back-translation \citep{sennrich2016backtranslation,edunov2018btLargeScale}, which generates synthetic examples by translating text into another language and then back into the original, often using a high-quality reverse model. While particularly successful in machine translation and cross-lingual transfer, it has also been adapted to other tasks such as classification, question answering, and paraphrase generation \citep{sugiyama2019btContextAware}.

Other effective methods modify the surface form of the input while preserving meaning. Lexical substitution techniques such as synonym replacement, random insertion, deletion, and swap (popularized by \emph{Easy Data Augmentation} \citep{wei2019eda}) are valued for their simplicity and domain-agnostic nature. Contextual augmentation \citep{kobayashi2018contextual} improves on these by leveraging pretrained language models to select contextually appropriate replacements, increasing fluency and reducing semantic drift.
\subsection{LLM-Based}
The rapid emergence of large language models (LLMs) has transformed synthetic data generation into a widely accessible and scalable capability. Whereas earlier augmentation techniques relied on handcrafted rules, back-translation, or small task-specific generators, modern LLMs can produce diverse, high-quality text directly from natural language prompts. This shift has enabled new paradigms where the model itself generates, refines, and diversifies examples without heavy human supervision.

More generally, LLMs have redefined the landscape of text data augmentation. Traditional approaches such as back-translation, paraphrasing, or task-specific generators often required substantial linguistic resources or domain-specific parallel corpora. In contrast, LLMs can produce diverse, high-quality, and contextually relevant data directly from prompts, enabling augmentation at scale and across domains without extensive manual intervention \citep{wangSelfInstructAligningLanguage2023,liSelfAlignmentInstructionBacktranslation2023,guptaTarGENTargetedData2024}. This paradigm shift has reduced reliance on human-authored seed data and opened possibilities for generating synthetic benchmarks, rare-event instances, and multilingual training material from scratch.

A variety of prompting and generation strategies have emerged to fully leverage LLM capabilities. Self-Instruct \citep{wangSelfInstructAligningLanguage2023} and Instruction Backtranslation \citep{liSelfAlignmentInstructionBacktranslation2023} bootstrap new instruction–response pairs from the model’s own outputs, improving instruction-following capabilities with minimal human input. Targeted generation methods such as TarGEN \citep{guptaTarGENTargetedData2024} employ multi-step prompting and self-correction to ensure label consistency and dataset diversity. Other techniques include prompt mixing \citep{schlegel2023promptmix}, paraphrase-based in-context learning augmentation \citep{li2023dail}, and domain-specific prompt engineering \citep{sahu2022intent_llm_offtheshelf}. Retrieval-augmented generation pipelines \citep{gao2024rag_survey,bonifacio2022inpars,saad-falcon2023udapdr} combine synthetic generation with relevant knowledge retrieval to enhance factual accuracy.

Taken together, these prompting and generation strategies provide the methodological foundation on which diverse applications of LLM-driven augmentation have been built. Beyond their methodological variety, they have been deployed across a wide range of NLP tasks. In cross-lingual scenarios, LLM-powered augmentation has improved zero-shot and few-shot performance by generating aligned multilingual pairs \citep{whitehouse2023llm_xling_aug,zhang2024llm_dep_parse_dialogue_cn}. For question answering, generation-based augmentation improves robustness to distributional shifts \citep{samuel2024lowres_rc,chowdhury2024qa_robustness_da}. Dialogue augmentation for emotional support \citep{augesc2023acl_findings} and grounded generation for knowledge-intensive tasks \citep{wu2021controllable_grounded_dialogue} further illustrate the breadth of capabilities unlocked by these methods.

Beyond general-domain NLP, similar strategies are increasingly being explored in healthcare, where data scarcity and privacy constraints amplify the value of synthetic augmentation. Recent systematic reviews confirm that generative AI is now being applied across multiple medical modalities, while also highlighting persistent challenges in factuality, diversity, and regulatory compliance \citep{Ibrahim2025}. At the task level, concrete implementations underscore these benefits: LLMs have been used to generate synthetic patient–trial matching examples \citep{yuan2023healthcare_da_trial_matching} and to transform conversational data into structured medical records \citep{pulsar_mediqasum_2023}. More specialised pipelines go further: MEDSAGE demonstrates that LLM-generated dialogues can significantly improve the robustness of clinical dialogue summarisation systems to ASR errors \citep{ramesh2025}, while Woo et al. show that distilling synthetic QA pairs enables smaller models to match or even surpass the performance of much larger models for large-scale clinical information extraction \citep{woo2025synthetic}. Together, these studies highlight how the methodological innovations of LLM-driven augmentation are not only broadly applicable across NLP, but also particularly well-suited to meet the factual and domain-specific demands of healthcare contexts.

Overall, LLM-based data augmentation has moved from a niche application to a mainstream technique, offering scalable, domain-adaptive, and instruction-driven synthetic data creation. The literature reflects a rapid diversification of methods, ranging from self-bootstrapping pipelines to retrieval-enhanced generation, underscoring the central role of LLMs in the next generation of data-centric NLP.
\subsection{LLM Generation with Feedback}

Recent advances in large language model (LLM)–driven data augmentation have increasingly incorporated feedback-guided generation, moving beyond static, single-pass outputs toward interactive refinement loops where a generator is paired with a critic. The critic’s role—whether realised as a textual reviewer, a scalar scorer, or a formal verifier—is to assess the generated content and guide the model toward improved outputs, often through multiple iterations. This paradigm blends elements of prompt engineering, self-improvement, and automated evaluation into a single framework, enabling greater control over factuality, style, and domain adherence.

A number of methods leverage textual feedback to make the refinement process interpretable and context-sensitive. Self-Refine \citep{madaan2023selfrefine} exemplifies this approach by having the model critique its own outputs in natural language before revising them, while Reflexion \citep{shinn2023reflexion} extends the loop with a memory of past successes and failures, effectively enabling long-term self-improvement. Self-Critique \citep{tan2023selfcriticism} integrates the critique directly into the reasoning chain, allowing the model to course-correct mid-generation rather than only in post-processing.

Other techniques favour scalar feedback, where quality is quantified as a numerical reward. While reinforcement learning from human feedback (RLHF) has been effective, its high annotation cost has motivated scalable alternatives such as RLAIF \citep{lee2023rlaif}, which replaces human evaluators with high-capacity LLMs acting as reward models. Similarly, Cappy \citep{li2023cappy} demonstrates that a small, specialised scorer can significantly enhance a large multi-task model by filtering generations in a “generate–score–select” loop. These scalar methods scale well to large candidate pools but trade off interpretability compared to textual critique.

In high-stakes applications like healthcare, verification-based feedback is particularly valuable. Rather than relying on subjective scoring, these approaches check correctness explicitly—either through symbolic rules or domain-specific LLM prompts. Divide–Verify–Refine \citep{zhang2024divideverifyrefine} breaks complex tasks into smaller, verifiable components, revising only those that fail checks, while SYNFAC-EDIT \citep{miller2024synfacedit} applies synthetic edit feedback to ensure factual alignment in clinical summarisation, addressing errors detected by a medical fact verifier.

Another influential strand, self-consistency filtering, aims to improve reliability by generating multiple reasoning paths and selecting the most frequent or mutually consistent answer. Introduced by \citet{wang2023selfconsistency}, this technique reduces random variability and has been extended in methods like Chain-of-Guidance \citep{raj2024chainofguidance}, which couples consistency checks with targeted verification queries.

Feedback can also be integrated directly at inference time without retraining. Approaches like Nudging \citep{fei2024nudging} dynamically adjust token probabilities based on a reward model’s evaluations, steering the generation toward desired attributes in real time. PAD (Personalised Alignment during Decoding) \citep{chen2025pad} further tailors outputs to specific user profiles or domain constraints during decoding, enabling rapid adaptation to new settings without costly fine-tuning.

Finally, critic–generator architectures formalise the feedback loop by explicitly separating the generator from the evaluator. This modularity allows specialised critics—such as those in CTRL \citep{zhang2024ctrl} for code generation—to enforce hard constraints, or even multi-agent debate systems where multiple generator–critic pairs argue over competing solutions. Closely related, LLM-as-a-Judge frameworks \citep{liu2023geval,fu2023gptscore} employ powerful LLMs as evaluators that score, rank, or select outputs, often with human-level agreement.

Synthetic data generation in the clinical domain has emerged as a promising yet challenging approach to mitigate data scarcity and privacy constraints in biomedical NLP. Early systematic evaluations, such as Tang et al. \citep{tang2023synthetic_clinical_llm}, demonstrate that large language models (LLMs) can produce labeled clinical data that improves tasks like named entity recognition and relation extraction, while also cautioning that naïve generation risks introducing distributional shifts detrimental to real-world performance. Xu et al. \citep{xu2023clingen} advance this idea with ClinGen, enriching prompts with structured ontology knowledge to generate more semantically coherent and clinically plausible examples.

Recent work increasingly incorporates feedback-guided generation to refine and control synthetic clinical text. For instance, SYNFAC-EDIT \citep{miller2024synfacedit} introduces an edit-based feedback loop, where factual inconsistencies in clinical summaries are detected and iteratively corrected, significantly improving factual alignment with source documents. Similarly, Woo et al. \citep{woo2025synthetic_distillation} adopt a distillation-based approach in which high-quality synthetic Q\&A pairs generated by an LLM are iteratively filtered and ranked by a domain-specific scorer before training smaller clinical extraction models. These methods illustrate how integrating scoring, factuality checks, or ontology-based validation into the generation process can curb hallucinations and preserve domain integrity.

Entirely synthetic training pipelines have also been explored. Asclepius \citep{kweon2024asclepius} is a clinical LLM trained solely on synthetic notes, while MedSyn \citep{kumichev2024medsyn} combines ontology-guided prompts with multi-pass feedback validation to create large corpora for code prediction tasks. Both approaches show that, when feedback mechanisms are carefully designed, fully synthetic data can yield competitive—sometimes superior—results compared to real-data baselines. Complementing these empirical advances, \citet{rao2025scoping_synthetic_clinical} review the state of synthetic data in clinical NLP, highlighting the growing adoption of feedback loops for quality control, as well as persistent challenges in factual accuracy, bias mitigation, and regulatory compliance.

Despite their promise, feedback-guided generation methods also present inherent risks when deployed in iterative or self-reinforcing settings. Recent studies have shown that unregulated feedback loops can lead to undesirable optimization behaviors, a phenomenon referred to as in-context reward hacking, where the model learns to exploit weaknesses in its scoring or guidance mechanisms rather than genuinely improving output quality \citep{perez2024feedbackloops}. Similarly, recursive reliance on a model’s own generations for further training has been found to induce model collapse, in which the output distribution progressively degenerates as diversity and factuality erode over iterations \citep{shumailov2024modelcollapse}. These findings underscore the importance of incorporating safeguards—such as diverse data refresh, cross-model feedback, or human-in-the-loop validation—when designing feedback-driven augmentation pipelines, particularly in safety-critical domains like clinical NLP.
	
\section{Alignment \& Self-Learning}

Reinforcement Learning (RL) is a learning paradigm where an agent interacts with an environment by generating actions and receives scalar feedback signals, called rewards, that indicate the desirability of its behavior. The goal of the agent is to learn a policy—a mapping from states to actions—that maximizes its expected cumulative reward over time. In the context of large language models, the “environment” corresponds to the text generation process, the “actions” are the successive tokens produced, and the “reward” encodes how well the generated text aligns with a desired objective. Building on this principle, Reinforcement Learning from Human Feedback (RLHF) adapts the RL framework by replacing hand-crafted or task-specific reward signals with human preference judgments \cite{christiano2017}. A landmark demonstration of this approach was introduced by \citet{ouyang2022training}, who showed that training language models through a three-stage pipeline—supervised fine-tuning, reward modeling, and reinforcement learning—enabled scalable instruction-following and significantly improved alignment with human expectations.

The alignment of large language models with human preferences has traditionally relied on RLHF, but this approach faces scalability and stability challenges due to its multi-stage pipeline. A first direction to address these issues is to reduce reliance on human annotators. For example, Reinforcement Learning from AI Feedback (RLAIF) uses a powerful AI model in place of human evaluators. Recent studies show that RLAIF can achieve competitive results to RLHF when using strong feedback models. In fact, \citet{lee2023rlaif} demonstrate that replacing human preference labels with GPT-4 feedback yields similar gains in summarization and dialogue tasks. However, \citet{archit2024criticalevaluation} provide a critical analysis of RLAIF, noting that some reported improvements stem from other training factors rather than the AI feedback alone, cautioning that RLAIF’s benefits may be context-dependent.

Beyond these algorithmic simplifications, it is worth recalling that RLHF was not initially designed for conversational alignment but rather introduced in the broader reinforcement learning literature as a way to provide intermediate reference signals for training agents in interactive or robotic environments. Early applications emphasized guiding agents in simulated settings, where human preferences offered sparse but crucial feedback for learning complex behaviors. The adaptation to language models repurposed this paradigm: instead of shaping motor actions or navigation strategies, preference judgments began to serve as alignment signals for textual outputs. This trajectory highlights how RLHF emerged from general reinforcement learning research before becoming the de facto approach for aligning large language models. As RLHF was ported to language modeling, however, its multi-stage structure—separating supervised fine-tuning, reward modeling, and policy optimization—proved computationally heavy and difficult to scale. This challenge motivated a wave of simplified preference optimization methods tailored specifically to text generation.

In parallel, researchers have proposed simplified preference optimization algorithms that streamline or even bypass the reinforcement learning step. Direct Preference Optimization (DPO) \citep{rafailov2023dpo} reframes alignment as a supervised learning problem on preference data, removing the need to train a separate reward model. Building on this idea, SimPO \citep{meng2024simpo} introduces an even simpler offline objective by using the model’s own average log-likelihood as an implicit reward and adding a margin between preferred and disfavored responses. This eliminates the need for a reference model and leads to more efficient, stable training. Concurrently, ORPO \citep{hong2024orpo} (Odds Ratio Preference Optimization) presents a monolithic alignment approach that combines supervised fine-tuning and preference alignment into a single stage. ORPO applies a log odds penalty to undesired (rejected) responses during training, requiring neither a separate SFT phase nor a reference model. These reference-free methods report improved or comparable alignment performance to classical RLHF while significantly reducing computational overhead. For example, SimPO achieves higher win rates than DPO on benchmarks like AlpacaEval 2 and Arena-Hard despite its simplicity.
Another recent contribution, KTO \cite{ethayarajh2024kto}, reframes alignment via prospect-theoretic utility maximization: instead of optimizing log-likelihoods over preference pairs, KTO defines a human-aware loss that incorporates non‑linear, risk‑sensitive utility functions modeled after prospect theory. It operates on simple binary feedback (desirable vs. undesirable) and has been shown to match or exceed DPO performance across model scales. While these reference-free methods improve efficiency, they remain largely offline and can suffer from overfitting to observed preference data, limiting their ability to generalize beyond the training distribution.

This observation has motivated a new line of work focusing on the exploration–efficiency trade-off in alignment. Exploratory Preference Optimization (XPO) \citep{xie2024xpo} addresses this by adding a principled exploration bonus to the Direct Preference Optimization (DPO) objective—a simple, one-line change. This bonus encourages the model to venture outside the support of the initial policy and preference data, fostering greater diversity and informativeness in generated outputs. Theoretically, XPO is provably sample-efficient and can converge to a near-optimal policy even when initial coverage is poor. Empirically, XPO demonstrates faster reward improvements compared to standard DPO variants, underscoring the essential role of exploration in preference alignment.

A second broad approach to alignment is to internalize the reward signal within the model itself. Instead of training an external reward model or using a separate critic, the model learns to judge and refine its own outputs. \citet{yuan2024selfrewarding} introduce Self-Rewarding Language Models, where the LM incorporates an internal “judge” and directly optimizes its outputs via Direct Preference Optimization (DPO) fine-tuning on its self-critiqued responses. This effectively makes the model its own reward provider, greatly reducing reliance on curated human feedback. \citet{zhou2025scir} extend this idea with Self-Consistent Internal Reward (SCIR), enforcing consistency among the model’s internal reward estimations to stabilize training. By ensuring the model’s self-critiques do not contradict each other, SCIR improves both alignment performance and the reliability of the learned reward model.

Hybrid methods that blend self-generated feedback with iterative fine-tuning have also shown promise. Reinforced Self-Training (ReST) \citep{gulcehre2023rest} are a framework that begin with a base model generating its own training data (e.g., question–answer pairs or dialogues), then iteratively fine-tune on these synthetic examples using a mix of supervised and reinforcement signals. Crucially, these methods incorporate a filtering or scoring step – sometimes using a smaller trained critic model as in \citet{li2023cappy} – to retain high-quality generations. Over successive rounds, the model “self-distills” better behaviors, achieving improvements without any human-labeled data.

Despite these advances, several limitations remain. Methods such as RLAIF and self-rewarding models raise concerns of circularity, since the same or closely related models are used both to generate and to evaluate outputs, potentially amplifying existing biases rather than correcting them. Even when internal critics are introduced, the calibration and reliability of reward signals remain fragile; approaches like SCIR explicitly attempt to mitigate inconsistencies in self-assessment, but these mechanisms are still in their infancy. More broadly, most preference optimization techniques operate in an offline setting, making them prone to overfitting the observed preference data and struggling to generalize beyond the training distribution. This limitation is especially problematic in domains such as clinical NLP, where data distributions are highly variable and out-of-distribution robustness is critical. These open challenges highlight that while the trajectory of alignment research is moving toward lighter, more autonomous pipelines, ensuring robustness, trustworthiness, and domain transferability remains an unresolved frontier.

Finally, recent works suggest that the trade-off at stake is not between RLHF and its simplifications alone, but between reinforcement-based optimization and supervised fine-tuning more broadly. Reinforcement learning methods tend to improve out-of-distribution robustness, but this often comes at the cost of reduced diversity and more monotonous generations \citep{kirk2024effects}. Such robustness may be valuable in heterogeneous settings where training and evaluation data diverge substantially, yet in other contexts the benefits of high-quality, carefully curated SFT data can outweigh those of reinforcement-based pipelines. Indeed, \citet{ye2025iterativelabel} and \citet{lin2024unlockingspell} highlight that targeted improvements in SFT—through iterative refinement or selective curation—can rival or even surpass RLHF, pointing to data quality as the true bottleneck in alignment.

\section{Privacy-Preserving Approaches in Sensitive Domains}
\subsection{Foundations of Privacy in Textual Data}
The concept of identification lies at the core of privacy-preserving data processing. In the context of textual data, identification refers to the ability to link a piece of information to a specific individual, either directly (e.g., through names, social security numbers) or indirectly (e.g., via quasi-identifiers such as date of birth or ZIP code)~\citep{sweeney2000simple}. De-identification broadly refers to any process that removes or obscures such identifiers to reduce the risk of re-identification. However, the term lacks standardization, and in practice, de-identification does not guarantee anonymity, as residual risks often persist through linkage or inference attacks~\citep{el2011systematic}.

Legal frameworks provide partially aligned but divergent interpretations of what constitutes sufficient anonymization. For instance, the GDPR distinguishes between anonymised data---which falls outside the scope of the regulation---and pseudonymised data, which remains regulated. The latter is defined in Article~4(5) of the General Data Protection Regulation (GDPR) as: \textit{``the processing of personal data in such a manner that the personal data can no longer be attributed to a specific data subject without the use of additional information, provided that such additional information is kept separately and is subject to technical and organisational measures to ensure that the personal data are not attributed to an identified or identifiable natural person''}~\citep{gdpr2016}. A key limitation of this approach is that the mapping key or auxiliary data may still be accessible to adversaries, particularly in institutional or cross-institutional data-sharing contexts~\citep{el2011systematic}.

By contrast, the HIPAA Privacy Rule in the United States defines de-identification using a more procedural approach, offering two distinct pathways: the Safe Harbor method, which requires the removal of 18 specific identifiers; and the Expert Determination method, in which a qualified expert must certify that the risk of re-identification is ``very small.'' As stated in §164.514(b)(1) of the HIPAA regulation: \textit{``the risk is very small that the information could be used, alone or in combination with other reasonably available information, by an anticipated recipient to identify an individual who is a subject of the information''}~\citep{hipaa_deid}.

Beyond legal definitions, privacy can also be framed through Helen Nissenbaum’s theory of *contextual integrity*, which argues that privacy is fundamentally about ensuring that flows of information occur in ways that are appropriate to their social context—not merely about preventing identifiability or securing control over data usage~\citep{nissenbaum2009privacy}. According to this theory, appropriateness depends on five defining parameters: the data subject, the sender, the recipient, the type of information, and the transmission principle (e.g., consent, confidentiality, legal obligation). Violations of privacy arise when an informational flow deviates from these contextual norms, even if explicit identifiers are absent or consent was technically obtained. This framework plays a dual role—it explains why certain practices (e.g., inappropriate sharing of clinical notes in non-clinical settings) feel privacy-violating and provides a normative standard for evaluating novel data practices. In NLP, privacy-by-design approaches inspired by this theory pay attention not only to token masking or pseudonymisation but also to the semantic role and situational legitimacy of information flows, aligning them with context-specific norms rather than focusing only on surface-level obfuscation.

\subsection{Anonymisation \& Pseudonymisation Techniques}

Anonymisation and pseudonymisation are two cornerstone techniques for mitigating privacy risks in the processing of sensitive textual data, especially in biomedical contexts. While often used interchangeably in practice, these two concepts differ significantly in both legal and technical terms.

Anonymisation refers to the process by which personal data is irreversibly altered in such a way that the data subject can no longer be identified, directly or indirectly, by any means reasonably likely to be used. In the GDPR framework, data that are truly anonymised are no longer classified as personal data and therefore fall outside the scope of data protection regulations, since the individuals they describe can no longer be identified.

By contrast, pseudonymisation involves replacing direct identifiers (e.g., names, social security numbers) with artificial tokens or placeholders, while retaining the ability to reverse the process via a mapping key. This makes the data still re-identifiable under certain conditions and thus subject to regulation. However, pseudonymisation is often preferred in practice due to its ability to preserve data utility, particularly in clinical and research settings.

Pseudonymisation is a widely adopted strategy for mitigating privacy risks in sensitive textual data, particularly in the biomedical domain. Rather than removing identifiable information entirely, pseudonymisation replaces such information with artificial identifiers or placeholders, thereby preserving document structure and semantics while limiting re-identification risks.

Early research by \citet{meystre2014impact} demonstrated that anonymisation procedures—such as the removal or replacement of personal identifiers—have minimal impact on the clinical utility of texts. Their evaluation showed that less than 3\% of medically relevant concepts were affected, providing initial empirical support for the feasibility of de-identified data reuse.

The field evolved significantly with the advent of neural approaches. \citet{dernoncourt2017rnn} pioneered the use of recurrent neural networks (RNNs) for automatic de-identification, demonstrating that deep learning models trained on clinical corpora could achieve state-of-the-art performance (F1 $>$ 0.97) without handcrafted features. This marked a turning point toward data-driven methods and away from static rule-based systems.

More recently, \citet{kovacevic2024review} conducted a systematic review covering 69 de-identification systems, tracing the evolution from rule-based methods to LSTM-based and transformer-based approaches. While modern models achieve high F1 scores on benchmark datasets, the authors caution that these systems often struggle to generalize to unseen data sources or low-resource settings, and call for more robust evaluation metrics.

Several recent studies have shifted focus toward the development and evaluation of pseudonymisation tools for real-world use. \citet{abuattieh2025tools} provided a comparative review of ten widely used pseudonymisation systems, categorizing them by scale, integration model, and intended deployment context. In follow-up work, \citet{attieh2024orchestra} introduced a scalable pseudonymisation tool, designed for rapid deployment in biomedical research networks using widely available office environments. The tool has been deployed across 13 institutions and processed over 100,000 patients, illustrating its operational viability.

Language-specific pseudonymisation has also gained traction. \citet{baumgartner2024masketeer} proposed Masketeer, an ensemble-based tool that combines rule-based entity masking with named entity recognition (NER) to pseudonymise German medical free-text documents. The system achieves over 93\% precision and recall while maintaining low computational complexity, highlighting the importance of language- and domain-specific adaptations.

Efforts to develop de-identification tools for low-resource languages have also emerged. \citet{berzi2025hungarian} presented a high-performing PII removal system for Hungarian EHRs using spaCy-based models. Their work underscores the feasibility of deploying NLP-based anonymisation pipelines in underrepresented linguistic contexts with sufficient annotation.

\citet{tannier2024aphp} report on the development of a large-scale pseudonymisation pipeline within the AP-HP clinical data warehouse. Their system adopts a hybrid design that integrates deep learning models with manually crafted rules: the former capture context-dependent identifiers such as names or clinical entities, while the latter reliably detect structured patterns such as dates, addresses, or numerical identifiers. This complementary combination ensures high recall without sacrificing precision. Evaluated across twelve categories of identifying entities, the pipeline achieves a global F1 score of 0.99. Beyond raw performance, the study examines how corpus size, document type, and model architecture influence results, and demonstrates the practical advantages of hybrid strategies for large-scale deployment. By releasing both the models and handcrafted rules under a permissive BSD 3-Clause license, the authors also emphasize reproducibility and adoption across institutions.

The integration of large language models (LLMs) into anonymisation and pseudonymisation workflows is a recent and promising direction. \citet{pissarra2024llm} compared LLM-driven anonymisation to traditional methods, proposing six new privacy metrics and finding that LLMs can achieve competitive results while preserving text utility. Similarly, \citet{jia2025twostage} proposed a two-stage anonymisation pipeline that combines entity extraction via LLMs with k-anonymity constraints, showing strong performance across several entity types.

In a notable advancement, \citet{staabLargeLanguageModels2025} introduced an adversarial anonymisation framework that leverages the inference capabilities of LLMs not only to evaluate privacy leakage but also to enhance anonymisation quality. Their experimental results—spanning 13 different LLMs and real-world datasets—show that this method outperforms both traditional and commercial anonymisers in terms of privacy and utility. Importantly, their human evaluation confirms a strong preference for the linguistic quality of LLM-anonymised texts.

Finally, \citet{patsakis2023gpt} raised critical concerns about residual re-identification risks. Their work demonstrated that even anonymised texts can leak sensitive identity clues through contextual inference, especially in the presence of powerful generative models like GPT. This highlights the need for anonymisation approaches that go beyond token-level substitution to account for semantic leakage and adversarial reasoning.

Taken together, these studies reflect a maturation of the field—from static rule sets to adaptive, multilingual, and context-sensitive anonymisation and pseudonymisation pipelines—while also highlighting ongoing risks posed by increasingly capable language models. Future work must balance regulatory compliance, data utility, and resilience against evolving adversarial capabilities.

\subsection{Differential Privacy}
Differential Privacy (DP) is one of the most widely adopted formal frameworks for quantifying and bounding privacy risk. As defined in \citet{dwork2014algorithmic}, DP provides a mathematical guarantee that the output of an algorithm remains essentially unchanged whether or not any single individual’s data is included in the input dataset. Formally, a randomized algorithm $\mathcal{A}$ satisfies ($\varepsilon$, $\delta$)-differential privacy if, for all adjacent datasets $D$ and $D'$ differing in one individual and for all measurable subsets S of possible outputs, it holds that: 
\[\Pr[\mathcal{A}(D) \in S] \leq e^\varepsilon \cdot \Pr[\mathcal{A}(D') \in S] +\delta\]

where $\varepsilon$ is a non-negative parameter that bounds the maximum privacy loss incurred by the presence or absence of any single individual in the dataset, and $\delta$ is a small probability representing the chance that this bound may be violated, allowing for a relaxation of the strict guarantee when exact differential privacy is unattainable.

The strength of differential privacy lies in its composability, resilience to post-processing, and its ability to provide quantifiable risk guarantees, making it a strong candidate for privacy-preserving machine learning. In the context of structured data analysis or tabular datasets, DP has seen widespread success.

However, in natural language processing, DP presents significant limitations. First, textual data is inherently high-dimensional, sparse, and semantically rich, which makes naive noise addition (e.g., through differentially private stochastic gradient descent) detrimental to both model performance and linguistic coherence \citep{li2022large,carlini2021extracting}. Second, measuring privacy leakage in textual outputs—such as summaries, paraphrases, or generated sentences—is far more challenging than in aggregate numerical queries. Finally, the assumption of bounded sensitivity (i.e., that one user’s data only marginally influences the output) does not hold naturally in generative text settings, where a single document can significantly steer model behavior.

As a result, while DP remains a foundational framework, its adaptation to NLP requires domain-specific strategies during fine-tuning, private synthetic data generation, or differential privacy at the embedding level. Ongoing work is seeking to balance utility and privacy in NLP applications without sacrificing semantic fidelity or task performance \citep{abadi2016deep,li2022large}.

\subsection{Differential Privacy for Language Models}

Differential Privacy (DP) has emerged as a foundational framework for formal privacy guarantees in machine learning. However, its integration with large language models (LLMs) poses unique challenges due to the high-dimensional, generative nature of textual data. A series of recent works has sought to address these challenges, either by adapting traditional DP mechanisms to the context of language models or by designing new paradigms tailored for text privatization tasks.

A first line of research focuses on applying DP-SGD to fine-tune pretrained LLMs. While this technique can severely impact model performance, it has been shown that using larger models and parameter-efficient fine-tuning strategies can mitigate the degradation. For instance, \citet{li2022large} demonstrated that a RoBERTa-Large model fine-tuned with DP-SGD can achieve near state-of-the-art results when paired with appropriate hyperparameters. Building on this, \citet{yu2022differentially} proposed a framework leveraging LoRA-style adapters and PEFT techniques to significantly reduce computational costs while improving the privacy-utility tradeoff.

Complementary to private training, an alternative strategy involves generating synthetic data with formal DP guarantees. This enables data sharing without exposing the original sensitive texts. \citet{mattern-etal-2022-differentially} demonstrated that fine-tuning GPT-2 with DP on clinical data could yield high-quality synthetic outputs that preserve semantic fidelity while preventing re-identification. Other works, like \citet{nahidSafeSynthDPLeveragingLarge2024} and \citet{xieDifferentiallyPrivateSynthetic2024}, focus on injecting noise into the data generation pipeline itself, leveraging LLM APIs and post-processing selection mechanisms to avoid training altogether, which is especially useful in settings where model access is restricted.

Prompt-based approaches represent another emerging category. These methods use LLMs in a zero-shot or few-shot setup to rewrite or generate privatized text. \citet{meisenbacherThinkingOutsideDifferential2024} compared three variants of DP prompting—strict, quasi-, and non-DP—revealing that non-strict methods often outperform fully DP methods in empirical evaluations. In a follow-up, \citet{meisenbacherJustRewriteIt2024} proposed a simple yet effective post-processing method that re-aligns the output of DP rewriting models with the original input, improving semantic similarity and privacy against author attribute inference attacks.

Several works explore DP in the context of in-context learning (ICL). \citet{tangPrivacyPreservingInContextLearning2023,tang2024privacy} proposed generating synthetic few-shot demonstrations under DP constraints, showing that such examples maintain downstream performance while significantly reducing prompt leakage risk. Similarly, \citet{duan2023flocks} introduced a differentially private prompt learning method that aggregates predictions from multiple noisy prompt learners, offering strong privacy guarantees in prompt selection.

In practical deployments, full retraining is often infeasible due to cost or data constraints. Addressing this, \citet{nageshPrivateTextGeneration2025} introduced the DP-KPS pipeline, which extracts keyphrases under DP from private texts and uses them to seed LLM prompts. This pipeline enables text generation without sharing sensitive data or requiring model access. \citet{utpalaLocallyDifferentiallyPrivate2023} proposed a complementary approach based on local DP and zero-shot prompting, where privacy is controlled via temperature-based generation, achieving strong de-anonymization resistance with minimal utility loss.

On the systems side, \citet{wangKnowledgeSGPrivacyPreservingSynthetic2024} presented KnowledgeSG, a hybrid architecture combining local DP training with server-side instruction generation and expert filtering. The system avoids sharing raw data and leverages federated learning principles to maintain privacy while improving model quality across clients.

Recent efforts also explore instruction-tuning and alignment under DP. \citet{yu2024privacy} proposed replacing human instructions with differentially private synthetic counterparts, enabling safe LLM alignment without exposing annotators or user data. \citet{shi-etal-2022-just} introduced Just Fine-tune Twice, a selective DP training pipeline that fine-tunes models on sanitized data, followed by a DP phase on the full dataset. This strategy retains high performance while protecting only sensitive tokens, offering a better utility-privacy tradeoff than standard DP.

Taken together, these works highlight the evolution of DP for LLMs from monolithic, high-overhead mechanisms toward modular, pragmatic pipelines that align privacy guarantees with task requirements. Across rewriting, prompting, fine-tuning, and generation workflows, a consensus emerges: DP can be both practical and effective when tailored to specific stages of the NLP pipeline and grounded in real-world threat models.

\section{Evaluation Criteria for Private Synthetic Data}
\subsection{Utility and Task Compatibility}

The utility of synthetic data is typically assessed through its capacity to support downstream task performance, such as named entity recognition (NER), relation extraction, question answering (QA), or summarization. While classical benchmarks such as GEM \citep{gehrmann2021gem} and hierarchical story generation \citep{fan2018hierarchical} remain instructive, recent work has emphasized clinical settings where task requirements are more stringent and domain-specific.

As detailed in Section~\ref{sec:resources}, modern benchmarks such as DRAGON, DiReCT, ArchEHR-QA, and MIMIC-IV-BHC provide robust evaluation settings for diverse tasks including diagnostic reasoning, document-level QA, and clinical summarization. These resources are particularly well-suited to test whether synthetic or augmented data preserves task-relevant signal and aligns with the linguistic and factual complexity of clinical notes.

\subsection{Semantic Coherence and Preference}
Assessing the quality of synthetic text goes beyond surface-level fluency or $n$-gram overlap. Two intertwined dimensions have emerged as central: semantic coherence, which captures internal consistency and meaningful alignment with the input, and preference-based evaluation, which reflects human or model judgments about the comparative desirability of outputs. Together, they move beyond rigid reference-matching to account for deeper aspects of quality such as meaning preservation, informativeness, and pragmatic usefulness.

Traditionally, semantic coherence has been estimated through lexical overlap metrics such as BLEU \citep{papineni2002bleu} and ROUGE \citep{lin2004rouge}, widely used in summarization and machine translation. However, these scores often fail to distinguish between semantically similar but lexically divergent outputs, or to penalize fluent yet incorrect generations. In response, embedding-based metrics like METEOR \citep{banerjee2005meteor}, BERTScore, and SEMScore \citep{aynetdinov2024semscore} offer more nuanced comparisons by incorporating contextual similarity at the sentence or discourse level. These methods are more sensitive to synonymy, paraphrasing, and subtle semantic shifts, yet still operate at the level of individual examples.

One widely used method for evaluating the quality of generated text is the Fréchet InferSent Distance (FID) \citep{heusel2017fid}. This approach begins by encoding a large set of real and generated texts into dense vectors using a sentence-level encoder such as InferSent. Each group of embeddings—real and generated—is then modeled as a multivariate Gaussian distribution in the latent space. FID quantifies the distance between these two distributions using the Fréchet distance (also known as the Wasserstein-2 distance). A lower FID score indicates that the generated texts follow a distribution closer to that of real texts, suggesting greater similarity in their overall statistical behavior.

MAUVE \citep{pillutla2021mauve} builds on similar intuitions but removes the simplifying Gaussian assumption. Instead, it directly compares the empirical distributions of real and generated text embeddings, typically obtained from a pretrained language model like GPT-2 or BERT. MAUVE introduces the concept of a divergence frontier, which traces the trade-off between precision (how much of the generated text lies within the support of the human distribution) and recall (how much of the human distribution is covered by the generated one). The area under this frontier defines the MAUVE score: the closer it is to 1, the more the generated distribution resembles that of real text across multiple levels of granularity.

Together, FID and MAUVE offer complementary perspectives for assessing text generation quality. Unlike surface-level metrics, they capture distributional-level alignment, helping detect issues such as mode collapse (where a model repetitively generates similar content) or off-topic drift—subtleties often missed by token-level comparisons.

As the semantic measures are limited in itself as they capture only the semantic similarities between a pair of document or a set of documents, the preference-based evaluation offers a complementary perspective. Rather than assessing outputs against references, it compares them pairwise—asking which one is better. This framing underpins large-scale systems like Chatbot Arena \citep{zheng2024chatbotarena} and AlpacaEval \citep{alpacaeval2023}, as well as modeling frameworks such as EloEval \citep{cao2024eloeval} and FaviScore \citep{vondaeniken2024faviscore}. These approaches, often using human judgments or LLM-based raters, aim to capture holistic dimensions of quality—coherence, helpfulness, informativeness—that are difficult to operationalize with reference-based scores.

Although preference-based evaluation is sometimes presented as capturing subjective utility or user satisfaction, it implicitly rewards semantic and pragmatic adequacy: outputs that are more relevant, consistent, or non-redundant tend to win comparisons. In this sense, preference signals constitute a disguised form of semantic judgment, emerging not from predefined metrics, but from comparative human-like reasoning.

In sum, semantic coherence and preference-based evaluation form two pillars of modern text evaluation: one grounded in meaning preservation and structural fidelity, the other in comparative quality and desirability. Combining both yields a more comprehensive and reliable framework for assessing the value of synthetic language, especially in open-ended tasks where rigid metrics fall short.

\subsection{Privacy Risk Assessment}

Privacy risk in synthetic text generation is typically assessed through empirical attacks that simulate adversarial settings to test whether a model or dataset leaks sensitive information. Among the most established evaluations are membership inference attacks, which aim to determine whether a given record was used in training. Foundational work has demonstrated that language models can memorize training data \citep{carlini2019secret_sharer}, with further refinements quantifying memorization across architectures and scales \citep{carlini2022quantifying_memo,schwarzschild2024rethinking_llm}. These findings have motivated the development of dedicated benchmarks such as \textsc{ProPILE} \citep{kim2023propile} and \textsc{PrivLM-Bench} \citep{li2024privlm_bench}, which operationalize privacy evaluation across multiple threat models.

In parallel, attribute inference attacks test whether a model’s outputs allow adversaries to reconstruct sensitive attributes of individuals. Black-box model inversion approaches have shown such inferences to be feasible in practice \citep{mehnaz2020blackbox_attribute_inference}, with recent work highlighting demographic disparities in vulnerability and proposing mitigation strategies \citep{kabir2025disparate_privacy}. These attacks reveal how leakage can persist even when datasets are synthetically generated or anonymized.

Re-identification and linkage attacks form a complementary class of privacy assessments, focused on whether a model’s outputs or a synthetic dataset can be matched to real individuals using auxiliary information. In the context of textual data, re-identification typically involves matching de-identified or synthetic samples to known individuals in an external dataset, based on overlapping identifiers (e.g., rare combinations of demographics, diagnosis codes, or location references). Studies such as \citet{manzanares2024reidentification_risk} have demonstrated that even anonymized clinical narratives can be vulnerable to such attacks through machine learning-based similarity scoring. Similarly, \citet{larbi2023clinical_reid} investigated how clinical text anonymization impacts both downstream NLP performance and re-identification risk, showing that de-identification is not always sufficient to prevent disclosure in practice.

Building upon this, linkage attacks generalize the notion of re-identification by attempting to associate records across datasets — for instance, linking a synthetic sample to a real-world patient profile by exploiting quasi-identifiers or semantic correlations. The state-of-the-art systematization by \citet{powar2023sok_linkage} outlines a formal threat model and provides concrete examples where partial overlaps (e.g., same admission date and condition) are sufficient to perform successful linkage. This is particularly concerning in healthcare and biomedical domains, where datasets often share structured fields (like ICD codes, timestamps, or age ranges) even after pseudonymization.

In these contexts, privacy evaluation requires both robust de-identification strategies and empirical testing against linkage-based adversaries. This motivates the need for evaluation frameworks that account not only for token-level identifiers but also latent semantic leakage and document-level structure, a direction now supported by recent benchmarks such as \citet{huang2024privacy_eval_nlp} and tools like \textsc{PrivLM-Bench}.
