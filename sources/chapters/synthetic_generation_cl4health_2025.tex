\chapter{Synthetic Documents for Medical Tasks: Bridging Privacy with Knowledge Injection and Reward Mechanism}
\label{chap:synthetic}

Generating synthetic clinical documents that preserve both privacy and utility remains a central challenge in medical \gls{nlp}.
Existing approaches often face a tension: methods prioritizing privacy tend to sacrifice clinical utility, while those preserving rich medical knowledge may leak identifying information through direct text exposure.
This chapter addresses this gap by proposing a generation methodology that decouples knowledge transfer from identity exposure.

Our core insight is that clinical knowledge can be transported through medical concept keywords extracted via ontologies, without requiring access to the original document text.
This raises two fundamental questions that guide our investigation: \textit{Are extracted keywords sufficient to convey the clinical knowledge necessary for downstream tasks?} and \textit{Can an iterative reinforcement mechanism, operating solely on numerical quality indicators, improve generation quality without compromising privacy?}

To answer these questions, we present a reward-based generation pipeline where only concept keywords and scalar scores cross the privacy boundary between hospital infrastructure and the public generation environment.
This work was published at the CL4Health workshop~\citep{meoni2025synthetic}; this chapter extends the original publication with an evaluation on the AlpaCare benchmark and comparisons with recent approaches such as KnowledgeSG.

\section{Introduction}

Electronic Health Records (\gls{ehr}) contain patient and healthcare staff interactions. Professionals record their impressions, observations, and various medical procedures performed. These notes remain fairly expressive and free to save healthcare personnel time and allow for the description of unusual situations \citep{rosenbloomDataClinicalNotes2011, wuSurveyClinicalNatural2022}. Natural Language Processing (\gls{nlp}) techniques speed up the decision processes~\cite{zhouDataSifterTextPartiallySynthetic2022,wuSurveyClinicalNatural2022}.

In recent years, Proprietary Online Large Language Models (\glspl{llm}) such as ChatGPT have shown impressive results using zero or few-shot techniques in analyzing these notes~\cite{Agrawal2022LargeExtractors, meoni2023large, hu2024improving}.

However, clinical \gls{nlp} faces challenges that arise from the sensitive, confidential, and specialized nature of its data—sending such patient information through an external \gls{api} raises numerous legal issues and is often impossible. Hospitals or third parties providing \gls{nlp}-based medical devices (i.e., directly impacting patient care) must maintain control over their \gls{nlp} systems to ensure patient safety. Therefore, the customization of open \glspl{llm} and their execution in a secure but computationally  and legally (i.e a model trained on a Hospital A can't be trained on a Hospital B) constrained environment is an important issue.

Still, specific training datasets are necessary to develop a model with clinical skills to address these challenges. To create such a dataset, obtaining real clinical data remains complicated and requires anonymization, which is time-consuming, expensive, and legally constrained. This also hinders the use of online models to annotate real data.

Alternatively, we propose to create synthetic clinical notes that look like real data but do not include personally identifiable Information (\gls{pii})~\cite{melamud_towards_2019, ive_generation_2020}. This approach has several benefits: it reduces the need for human input, complies with regulations, and is suitable for annotation with external models to train local models. The local models and datasets can be shared with the community without leaking confidential information. These local models are also small enough to be hosted inside the hospital's infrastructure.

Considering these issues, we implement a novel method for generating synthetic documents, enforcing privacy preservation by design, using only a tiny seed set of pseudo-anonymised data. As a proof of concept, our key contributions include:

\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Privacy-safe Document Generation guided by Clinical Knowledge and Reward Mechanism:} We present a methodology that leverages a minimal set of manually pseudo-anonymized data to train fine-tuned generative models. As illustrated in Figure~\ref{fig:workflow}, our approach operates across two distinct environments: a private area (hospital infrastructure) containing sensitive patient data, and a public area where synthetic documents are generated and refined. The workflow begins with keyword extraction from real documents using QuickUMLS \citep{soldainiQuickUMLSFastUnsupervised2016}, which extracts only clinical concepts without any \gls{pii}. The generation process is enhanced by enriching prompts with these clinical knowledge keywords, as detailed in Section~\ref{sec:experiment}. Furthermore, we improve the quality of synthetic documents through an iterative refinement process that employs a private scorer to compare real and synthetic documents. Critically, this scorer returns only numerical scores (floats) to the public side, ensuring privacy while enabling continuous improvement of the synthetic document quality through alignment techniques. This architecture maintains strict privacy guarantees by ensuring that no sensitive information crosses the boundary from the private to the public environment.

\item \textbf{Proof of Concept using \gls{mimic}-III:} Because it's almost impossible to evaluate our methods on real private documents, we utilize the \gls{mimic}-III clinical notes \citep{Johnson2016MIMIC-IIIDatabase} as a proxy to simulate a private healthcare environment, demonstrating our method's potential in a controlled setting. This proof of concept illustrates how our methodology could be applied in real-world hospital scenarios without compromising patient data.

\item \textbf{Evaluation on downstream tasks using \gls{mimic}-III:} To assess the quality of the synthetic documents as training dataset for smaller models, we evaluate the generated data using two tasks: Multilabel Classification based on \gls{icd}-9 Codes (\gls{icd}-MC) and Synthetic Named Entity Recognition (\gls{ner}). For \gls{icd}-MC, based on the codes proposed by \citet{mullenbach_explainable_2018} and \gls{mimic}-III manual annotations, we have modified this task, as described in Section~\ref{subsec:multitask}, to compare the performance of the model trained with real data against the model trained with synthetic data. The \gls{ner} task is conducted on annotations returned by GPT-4 on both our synthetic and real data. This allows us to compare the performance of models trained on these datasets.
\end{itemize}

\section{Related Works}

\paragraph{Synthetic Data Generation:}
Many recent studies focus on creating synthetic data, particularly for generating clinical data. For instance,~\citet{kweon2024asclepius} proposes to train \glspl{llm} for different purposes using synthetic clinical data generated by online \glspl{llm}. \citet{xieDifferentiallyPrivateSynthetic2024} has developed AUG-PE, a high-quality differential privacy synthetic text generation method leveraging \gls{api} access.

Furthermore, the work by~\citet{li_synthetic_2024} introduces Generalized Instruction Tuning (GLAN). Unlike previous approaches that rely on seed or existing datasets, GLAN uses a pre-curated taxonomy of human knowledge and capabilities as input to generate instructions across all disciplines. Inspired by their method, our work uses ontological information to extract sequences of ontology-based keywords from texts.

To assess the performance of \gls{llm} in Multiple Questions Choices in the medical field, \citet{griot_multiple_2024} developed a fictional medical benchmark to isolate the knowledge of the \gls{llm} from its test-taking abilities. \citet{liTwoDirectionsClinical2023} generated a synthetic dataset of Alzheimer's Disease relative signs. As this task is relatively complex, \gls{llm} created the dataset by incorporating expert knowledge taxonomy. Finally, the \citet{hiebelCanSyntheticText2023, xieDifferentiallyPrivateSynthetic2024} works focus on generating a synthetic dataset of clinical cases for the \gls{ner} task to study the effectiveness of real clinical data versus synthetic data.

\paragraph{Self-Rewarding:}
Reinforced Self-Training is an offline \gls{rl} algorithm proposed by~\citet{gulcehre2023rest} for self-align \glspl{llm} generating a dataset from the initial \gls{llm} policy and using it to improve the policy via offline \gls{rl}. Instruction back translation \cite{liSelfAlignmentInstructionBacktranslation2023} is a scalable method that automatically labels human-written text with corresponding instructions by finetuning a \gls{lm} on a small seed dataset and a web corpus to generate and selecting high-quality examples for further finetuning. \citet{yuan2024selfrewarding} use the trained \gls{llm} to provide rewards via \gls{llm}-as-a-Judge prompting, improving both instruction following and reward provision. \citet{lee2023rlaif} introduces Reinforcement Learning from \gls{ai} Feedback (\gls{rlaif}) as an alternative, using an off-the-shelf \gls{llm} to generate preference labels. \gls{rlaif} achieves comparable or superior performance to \gls{rlhf} in many tasks, such as those rated by humans.

The difference from the other approaches to generating a synthetic dataset is that our method combines \gls{llm} guided by prompts enriched with clinical knowledge, fine-tuned with a low amount of real pseudonymized data, and reinforcement learning feedback. This feedback is based on a score, which compares the real and synthetic data to ensure that they are closer to the source while maintaining privacy, as illustrated in Algorithm~\ref{algorithm}.

We sketch the main steps of our reward-based generation process, illustrated with Algorithm~\ref{algorithm}.

\subsection{Collecting keywords}

The generation of synthetic \crt{\textit{s}} is guided by prompts enriched with clinical knowledge represented by non-confidential \gls{umls} concepts ($C$) (Figure \ref{experiment:prompt}) extracted from real documents. Of course, other sources of keywords are possible. Therefore, our first processing step is to extract such keywords from each real document of dataset \dgen, collecting them in \kgen

\subsection{Seed Step}

We sample a tiny seed subset \dsft (i.e., supervised fine-tuning) from \dgen, and associated keyword sequences \ksft, with a ratio of $\rsft\%$. This seed subset is assumed to be carefully pseudo-anonymized to authorize its use to finetune our initial public generator model \mg. In our case, one or two hundred pseudo-anonymized documents suffice.

\subsection{Generation Step}
For each keyword sequence in \ktr$~=~$\kgen$\setminus$\ksft and generation~$r$, the generator model \mg generates $N>1$ candidate documents, collected in dataset $\DGen{step}$. This way, each synthetic document has a real counterpart based on the same sequence of keywords. In practice, we set $N=4$.

\subsection{Scoring Step}
\label{subsec:scoring-step}

We evaluate the quality of the generated documents using \score \cite{aynetdinov2024semscore}, a metric based on semantic textual similarity (STS) returned by our private evaluator model \me. The key point is that the \me must be hosted in a private infrastructure to compare public synthetic documents with real private ones.

In Algorithm~\ref{algorithm}, we use a light orange background colour to indicate that this step takes place on the private side of the hospital building. However, being only composed of floats, the score set $D_{score}$ can be safely declassified and returned from the private side to the public one for the Alignment step to train safely a new updated version of public \mg.
At the first generation step ($step = 0$), we initialize \me, fine-tuning it with a contrastive objective, selecting a subset of $\DGen{0}$ to serve as negative examples and their real counterparts as positive examples. 

Using \me, we score the $N$ candidates of each group from \dgstar against their counterparts in \dtr. We keep only the best groups whose highest score is above the $p^{th}$ percentile. In practice, we set $p = 80$. 

In each kept group, the candidate with the highest score (resp. lowest one) is selected as the \textit{chosen} (resp.  \textit{rejected}) candidate. Finally, a dataset \ddpo is formed from these selected candidate pairs.

 
\subsection{Alignment Step}
Using dataset \ddpo, we align and update \mg with \textit{DPO} (Direct Preference Optimization) \citep{rafailov2023dpo}.

\section{Experiments}
\label{sec:experiment}

Beyond generation quality, a central goal is to evaluate whether facsimile documents can serve as effective training data for downstream clinical \gls{nlp} tasks.
This evaluation serves a dual purpose: first, it measures the utility of synthetic documents for tasks close to real hospital use cases, such as \gls{icd} coding and entity extraction; second, it validates the scenario in which small, efficient models are trained on synthetic data and deployed within hospital infrastructure, where computational resources and data governance constraints make large models impractical.
Moreover, since facsimile documents are non-confidential, they can be freely annotated by \glspl{llm} without privacy concerns, an approach we explore in depth in \autoref{chap:distillation}.
In practice, downstream evaluations require test sets composed of real, manually annotated documents, and must be conducted in a secure environment.

\subsection{Base Models}
We use \space\verb|Mistral-7B-Instruct-v0.1|\space\citep{jiangMistral7B2023} as our base generator model, a trade-off between performance and computational cost. As an evaluator model, we use \space\verb|all-distilroberta-v1|.

\subsection{Dataset}
\label{subsec:dataset}
We use a dataset from \mimic as a proof of concept, involving pre-processing, keyword extraction, and post-processing.
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textit{Pre-processing}: We extract from \mimic the clinical notes from the clinical event row. We select only the \textit{Discharge Summaries} from these clinical notes and parse them to retrieve the \textit{History of Patient Illness} section, using them as documents for \dgen. On average, the documents consist of 248 words.
    
    \item \textit{Knowledge enrichment}: We project \gls{umls} concepts using QuickUMLS over \dgen. QuickUMLS is an unsupervised biomedical concept extraction based on pattern matching that guarantees only medical concepts are extracted and no identifying information. We obtain \kgen (cf. Section~\ref{sec:method}) used to enrich the prompts, as illustrated in Figure~\ref{experiment:prompt}. On average, we extract 58 keywords per document.
    
    \item \textit{Post-processing}: We filter out documents without keywords. We keep ordered keywords to encourage the model to follow the same narrative as the ground truth.
    In this way, we constitute a dataset of \num{4262} documents, using 70\% of them (\num{2581}) as a train set (\dtr) and 30\% (\num{1680}) as a test set (\dte). Moreover, the \dsft with 4\% and 6\% ratios have \num{156} and \num{235} documents, respectively.
\end{enumerate}

\section{Evaluation on Downstream Tasks}

\subsection{Multilabel Classification tasks}
\label{subsec:multitask}
\paragraph{Collecting Gold Annotations:}
As \mimic includes a set of expert-labeled \gls{icd}-9 codes ($L$) for each discharge summary, we use these annotations to:
\begin{enumerate}
\item evaluate the quality of our datasets on tasks close to a real use-case
\item test across a series of \gls{icd}-MC tasks with increasing complexity.
\end{enumerate}
We establish an association between these labels and the data points in \dtr and \dte, respectively, \num{2581} and \num{1681} data points.

We get annotated datasets (~\dtr,~\ltr~) and (~\dte,~\lte~) by coupling documents with labels. In defining our series of \gls{icd}-MC tasks, we prioritize the most frequent $k$ labels, denoted as class-k (see Table~\ref{tab:datasets-proportion}) with $k \in \{20,50,100,400\}$. We subsequently refine  (~\dtr,~\ltr~) and (~\dte,~\lte~) by retaining only those documents whose labels intersect with the set of \class{k} labels.

We define the refined training set as \dgold~$=$~(~\dptr,~\lptr~) where each document in \dptr contains at least one label from \class{k}. Documents devoid of any intersecting labels are excluded. Table~\ref{tab:datasets-proportion} presents the dataset sizes, which document the number of excerpts retained after applying these exclusion criteria.

It should be noted that the task's complexity increases with $k$ not only because of the larger set of labels and the lower frequency of some labels but also because of the longer label set on average per document. For instance, the average length is around 6 when $k=20$ but 11 when $k=100$.

\paragraph{Constituting the Synthetic Train Datasets: }
As an approximation, we hypothesize that the synthetic data point from \dgstar, which shares the same set of \gls{umls} keywords as its real data counterpart, can inherit the same set of \gls{icd} labels \lptr. This way, we easily obtain six synthetic datasets, denoted as $\DGen{step}$, corresponding to the generation steps $step \in \{0,1,2\}$ and seed ratios $r \in \{4\%,6\%\}$, as shown in Table~\ref{tab:main-results}. Each $\DGen{step}$ dataset contains four times more document data points than \dgold.

\subsection{Named Entity Recognition (\gls{ner}) Task}
\paragraph{Annotating the Overall Dataset:}

Because \mimic does not include gold \gls{ner} annotations, we use GPT-4 to automatically annotate all (synthetic and real) train and test datasets (\citet{noauthor_gpt-4_nodate}), focusing on three entity types: \textbf{problem}, \textbf{treatment} and \textbf{test}. We employ a few-shot learning approach inspired by \citet{hu2024improving}, using the prompt in Figure~\ref{experiment:prompt}. This use of \gls{llm}-based annotation on synthetic documents is possible precisely because facsimile documents are non-confidential; we study this approach in greater depth in \autoref{chap:distillation}. To assess whether or not the annotated entities are essentially the \gls{umls} keywords, we evaluated the overlap between keywords and annotations and found a low $22.36\%$ overlap.

Table~\ref{tab:datasets-proportion} illustrates the distributions of labels for the \gls{icd}-MC tasks and entities for \gls{ner}.

\subsection{Training of Task Models}
We train a series of (small) \verb|deberta-v3-base| \cite{heDeBERTaDecodingenhancedBERT2021} models on \gls{icd}-MC tasks using either real or synthetic datasets  \dgold or $D_{step}^{r}$ over the four tasks \class{k} where k $\in \{20,50,100,400\}$.

To address the quantity bias of a larger synthetic dataset, we train two baseline models, one trained with \dgold, and another one trained with \dgover, where each real document is oversampled $N=4$ times, hence containing the same amount of documents as the synthetic set.

We also consider a \baseline where only keywords (\ktr) are used to predict labels to check that the content of the documents impacts the performance, as shown in Table~\ref{tab:main-results}.

We apply the same methodology for the \gls{ner} task but with only \dgold and \dgover as baselines.

\section{Results}
Table~\ref{tab:semscores} presents a comparative analysis of \score measurements by evaluators across the different datasets generated at various steps.
Two trends emerge clearly.
First, scores increase consistently with each \gls{dpo} iteration: for instance, $\GenMdl{6\%}$ improves from $67.26$ at step~0 to $74.37$ at step~2 when evaluated by $\ScoreMdl{6\%}$, confirming that iterative alignment effectively refines the quality of generated documents.
Second, the $\GenMdl{6\%}$ model outperforms $\GenMdl{4\%}$ at every step, indicating that a larger seed ratio produces higher-quality synthetic documents.

\section{Extended Evaluation on AlpaCare Benchmark}
\label{sec:alpacare-eval}

Since our method shares several conceptual similarities with KnowledgeSG \cite{wangKnowledgeSGPrivacyPreservingSynthetic2024}, we sought to establish a comparison with this approach. KnowledgeSG (\autoref{fig:knowledgesg-workflow}) proposes a client-server framework where the client possesses private knowledge (for example, a clinical model trained on sensitive data) while a knowledge distillation process occurs via a remote professional model. Like our pipeline, this method adopts an iterative approach and integrates alignment mechanisms and sample selection: it generates multiple candidates then progressively filters them via a reference professional model to select the best samples, thus countering the performance degradation induced by gradient noise when using differential privacy.
Crucially, differential privacy serves as the foundational privacy mechanism in KnowledgeSG: the local model is fine-tuned using DP-SGD, which provides formal privacy guarantees by bounding the influence of any single training example through gradient clipping and noise injection.
This DP fine-tuning step is what enables the subsequent knowledge distillation to occur safely, as the local model's outputs are provably privacy-preserving.
However, the iterative goals differ between the two approaches: in KnowledgeSG, the iterative process aims to denoise the DP-degraded model through teacher-guided knowledge distillation, whereas our approach uses an iterative alignment process based on semantic similarity to progressively guide the generator towards producing higher-quality synthetic documents.
It is worth noting that, based on our exchanges with the authors of KnowledgeSG, the iterative mechanism described in their paper does not yield significant improvements in practice: only a single iteration proves effective, with subsequent iterations failing to improve generation quality.

\begin{figure}[htp!]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=1\textwidth]{figures/synthetic_generation_cl4health_2025/knowledgesg_workflow.png}
\caption{Overview of the KnowledgeSG system architecture~\citep{wangKnowledgeSGPrivacyPreservingSynthetic2024}. A local base model ($\mathbb{W}_{Loc}$) learns knowledge from private data on the client side through DP fine-tuning, then acquires knowledge distillation from a professional model ($\mathbb{W}_{Pro}$) on the server side, producing a final target model ($\mathbb{W}_{Target}$) fine-tuned on high-quality synthetic data.}
\label{fig:knowledgesg-workflow}
\end{figure}

This methodological convergence naturally led us to adopt the AlpaCare benchmark \cite{zhang2023alpacare}, a doctor-patient conversational dataset in chatbot format, used both as a reference professional model in KnowledgeSG and as an evaluation benchmark in our context.
The downstream task here is medical question answering: given a patient question, the model must generate a clinically relevant and accurate response.
This choice presents a dual advantage: on one hand, it enables direct comparison with KnowledgeSG on common evaluation ground, and on the other hand, it addresses a different type of task from our usual evaluations (document-level classification and entity extraction), thus broadening the validation spectrum of our approach to document quality assessment.
The evaluation follows a preference evaluation protocol where a judge compares pairwise the responses generated by our model with those from established reference models (text-davinci-003 \cite{OpenAI2022TextDavinci003}, GPT-3.5-turbo \cite{OpenAI2022GPT35Turbo}, GPT-4 \cite{noauthor_gpt-4_nodate}, and Claude-2 \cite{Anthropic2023Claude2}). To ensure result robustness, each sample is evaluated twice with swapped positions to avoid positional bias.

For our approach to adapt to this benchmark, we modified our method as follows: our SynthKG pipeline specifically generates synthetic patient questions from extracted medical keywords, while corresponding responses are produced by AlpaCare, which serves as the reference professional model. The process is structured as follows: we first extract clinical keywords, from which we synthesize patient questions, then AlpaCare generates the associated expert responses. This combination produces a question-answer pair dataset that is then used to fine-tune a model capable of answering questions from the AlpaCare dataset during evaluation.

To enable a fair comparison, we aligned the experimental conditions of our pipeline with those of KnowledgeSG.
Both methods use the same private dataset of \num{500} samples from the AlpaCare benchmark dataset and identical differential privacy parameters ($\varepsilon = 8$).
The main difference lies in the candidate generation and selection strategy: KnowledgeSG generates \num{1000} candidates (twice the private dataset size) and filters them down to approximately \num{500} using a cosine similarity threshold, whereas our pipeline generates \num{5000} candidates and selects the \num{1500} best based on \score ranking or multi-criteria evaluation (\autoref{subsec:multi-criteria}).
Both pipelines use a single iteration.

\section{Candidate Selection Strategies}
\label{sec:quality-eval}

Given the results obtained with our initial pipeline, we identified potential improvement margins in the quality of synthetic document selection. While SemScore provided a foundation for semantic similarity assessment, we hypothesized that more sophisticated evaluation mechanisms could better capture the nuanced requirements of high-quality clinical synthetic documents. This section explores multi-faceted quality evaluation approaches that go beyond simple semantic similarity (See also \autoref{chap:french-application}).

\subsection{Medical Domain Filtering}

The first quality enhancement we implemented was a medical domain filtering process adapted from KnowledgeSG \cite{wangKnowledgeSGPrivacyPreservingSynthetic2024}. This filter addresses a fundamental quality requirement: ensuring that the generated synthetic documents remain medically relevant and do not drift into non-clinical domains during the generation process. The filtering mechanism operates by identifying whether a generated document or example constitutes a candidate whose subject matter falls within the medical domain.

This filtering step serves multiple purposes. First, it discards off-topic or non-medical generations that may arise from the stochastic nature of large language models, particularly when keyword guidance is sparse or ambiguous. Second, it ensures that synthetic documents maintain clinical relevance, a critical requirement for downstream medical \gls{nlp} tasks. Third, it provides a coarse-grained quality gate before more expensive evaluation mechanisms are applied, reducing computational costs by filtering out obviously unsuitable candidates early in the pipeline.

The medical domain classifier was trained to distinguish clinical text from general domain text, focusing on lexical markers, medical terminology density, and structural patterns characteristic of clinical documentation. While simple in concept, this filter proved effective at eliminating generations that deviated significantly from the expected clinical context.

\subsection{\gls{llm}-as-Judge Scoring}

Following medical domain filtering, we explored advanced \gls{llm}-as-judge evaluation techniques \cite{archit2024criticalevaluation,lee2024selfjudge}. This approach represents a paradigm shift from traditional automatic metrics by leveraging the contextual understanding and reasoning capabilities of large language models to evaluate document quality.

The \gls{llm}-as-judge framework operates by providing a large language model with a carefully designed prompt that instructs it to evaluate synthetic documents along multiple quality dimensions. Unlike SemScore, which measures semantic similarity through embedding distance, or traditional metrics like \gls{bleu} or \gls{rouge}, which rely on surface-level token overlap, \gls{llm}-as-judge can assess higher-level qualities such as clinical coherence, factual plausibility, narrative flow, and adherence to medical documentation standards.
A key distinction is that \gls{llm}-as-judge evaluates synthetic documents in isolation, without requiring access to the original real documents. This makes it particularly suited to our privacy-preserving context, where direct comparison with real patient records is not always feasible. However, this also means that the evaluation captures intrinsic document quality rather than fidelity to a specific real counterpart.

We designed evaluation prompts that asked judge models to score documents on a 1-5 scale based on clinical relevance, coherence, and appropriateness. The prompts explicitly instructed the model to consider whether the document could plausibly represent a real clinical note, whether the medical information presented was internally consistent, and whether the temporal sequence of events made clinical sense. We applied this scoring to each synthetic candidate independently.

The advantage of \gls{llm}-as-judge lies in its ability to capture nuanced quality aspects that are difficult to formalize in traditional metrics. However, this approach introduces new challenges: judge models may exhibit biases, their evaluations may not perfectly align with human expert judgment, and the computational cost of \gls{llm} evaluation is substantially higher than traditional metrics. Furthermore, the reliability and calibration of \gls{llm} judges remains an active research question, particularly when evaluating specialized medical content.

\subsection{Multi-Criteria Selection Pipeline}
\label{subsec:multi-criteria}

Our composite evaluation strategy combined medical filtering and \gls{llm}-as-judge in a two-stage pipeline. First, medical domain filtering eliminated clearly unsuitable candidates, reducing the evaluation pool. Second, \gls{llm}-as-judge provided fine-grained quality scoring on the filtered candidates. This hierarchical approach aimed to balance computational efficiency with evaluation sophistication.

We also explored weighted combination strategies where multiple quality signals—SemScore, medical domain probability, and \gls{llm}-judge scores—were aggregated to produce a final quality estimate. The intuition was that different metrics capture complementary aspects of quality: SemScore measures semantic fidelity to real documents, medical filtering ensures domain relevance, and \gls{llm}-as-judge assesses higher-level coherence and plausibility.

\section{Results and Analysis}

The detailed results presented in Table~\ref{tab:alpacare_detailed_results} reveal several important observations regarding our pipeline's performance. Analyzing first the results without medical filtering (using only SemScore), our SynthKG variants achieve respectable performance: the \gls{dpo}-1 variant reaches 0.520 on average, while the \gls{sft} variant obtains 0.510. These scores position us competitively, significantly surpassing DP-Instruct-\gls{icl} (0.266), which according to the literature represents the best of traditional differential privacy methods.

The combined application of medical filter and \gls{llm}-as-judge (upper section of Table~\ref{tab:alpacare_detailed_results}) shows mixed results: DP-\gls{sft} + \gls{kto} reaches \num{0.540} and DP-\gls{sft} + \gls{dpo} \num{0.528}.
These more elaborate evaluation techniques consistently improve over the SemScore-only variants, with DP-\gls{sft} + \gls{kto} gaining nearly two points (\num{0.521} to \num{0.540}).
This confirms that multi-criteria filtering does capture quality dimensions that SemScore alone misses, even if the additional complexity limits the magnitude of the gain.
On the overall average, these results remain below KnowledgeSG (\num{0.562}) and AlpaCare (\num{0.536}).
However, a closer examination of the per-evaluator results reveals that this ranking is largely driven by a single model: Text-davinci-003, where KnowledgeSG achieves an exceptionally high win rate of \num{0.776}.
When excluding Text-davinci-003 from the average, the picture changes substantially: our best variant DP-\gls{sft} + \gls{kto} achieves \num{0.498}, outperforming both KnowledgeSG (\num{0.492}) and AlpaCare (\num{0.492}).
This suggests that our approach is more robust across stronger, more recent evaluator models.
Against GPT-4 and Claude-2 specifically, DP-\gls{sft} + \gls{kto} obtains win rates of \num{0.492} and \num{0.490}, compared to \num{0.457} and \num{0.488} for KnowledgeSG.
The disproportionate advantage of KnowledgeSG on Text-davinci-003 is noteworthy but difficult to interpret: this older, smaller model may be more easily influenced by surface-level features of the generated text, though we cannot investigate this hypothesis further as we do not have access to the KnowledgeSG generated data.

The limited magnitude of these improvements raises important questions about the cost-effectiveness of more complex evaluation approaches. We propose two hypotheses to explain these results. First, the bootstrap phase may be insufficient: our initial supervised fine-tuning on a small seed set may not provide enough diversity for the generator to benefit from more sophisticated selection mechanisms. Second, the evaluation techniques themselves may not provide sufficiently discriminative signals for this specific domain: medical conversational text may require domain-specific evaluation criteria that general-purpose \gls{llm} judges cannot adequately capture.

Our exchanges with the authors of KnowledgeSG also revealed that certain figures from the original paper should be taken with caution, identifying potential problems in how results were constructed. The comparison between filtered and unfiltered versions thus suggests that while quality-oriented evaluation improvements offer theoretical advantages, their practical benefits in our specific pipeline remain moderate, highlighting the need for further investigation into domain-specific evaluation mechanisms tailored to medical synthetic data generation.

\section{Discussion}

Besides validating our privacy-safe generation process, our results, obtained on both \gls{mimic}-III and the AlpaCare benchmark, have provided crucial insights into the impact of both the quality and quantity of synthetic training data on the performance of encoder models. \textbf{It is evident that refining the generator through \gls{dpo}, using clinical concepts as inputs, enhances the synthetic dataset's quality, especially when the first alignment step has been performed}.

Results indicate that training models on synthetic data not only preserves but outperforms models trained on gold datasets, as illustrated in Table~\ref{tab:main-results}.
This highlights the potential of using privacy-preserving synthetic documents to maintain high data utility while protecting sensitive information.

\textbf{The accuracy of the \score scoring mechanism as a predictor of data quality for downstream tasks is also particularly pronounced}. The nature of tasks significantly influences the predictive quality, as shown in Figure~\ref{fig:correlation-f1-semscore}. The need for text closely aligned with the source material to ensure accurate identification of rarer labels was clear, highlighting \score's role as a critical metric in evaluating and refining the quality of synthetic documents.

\textbf{Extended evaluation on different benchmarks and quality mechanisms further validates our approach}. The AlpaCare benchmark evaluation (Section~\ref{sec:alpacare-eval}) demonstrates that our method generalizes beyond traditional clinical note tasks to conversational medical settings.
While KnowledgeSG achieves the highest overall average, this advantage is concentrated on the weakest evaluator model (Text-davinci-003); when evaluated by stronger, more recent models (GPT-4, Claude-2), our best variant DP-\gls{sft} + \gls{kto} outperforms both KnowledgeSG and AlpaCare, suggesting greater robustness of our approach.
The exploration of advanced quality evaluation methods (Section~\ref{sec:quality-eval}), including medical domain filtering and \gls{llm}-as-judge mechanisms, confirms that multi-criteria filtering consistently improves over SemScore alone, though the magnitude of the gain remains moderate.
These findings suggest that our core methodology captures the essential quality dimensions, with room for further improvement through domain-specific evaluation criteria.

While increasing the dataset size improves performance, applying selective filtering strategies, such as percentile sampling, on a larger volume further enhances results, surpassing the model trained with \dgold. These findings suggest that both data quantity and quality can be adjusted to optimize outcomes, as highlighted in Figure~\ref{fig:samplings}.

Another interesting finding is that we can concatenate the datasets generated on the different $steps$ to increase performance. This is illustrated in overall tasks, where diversity is improved by using more data and simulating a more diverse dataset through the heterogeneous data quality, outperforming the model trained with \dgover.

\section{Conclusion}
We deliver a method for generating synthetic privacy-safe documents. Our method consists of:
\begin{enumerate}
\item initializing the model with a small number of pseudo-anonymized documents, which reduces the need for human input
\item employing a private evaluator to score the generated document against real documents, preserving the confidentiality of the data while ensuring proximity between real and synthetic documents
\item validating the approach across multiple benchmarks and evaluation metrics, from traditional clinical coding tasks to conversational medical datasets.
\end{enumerate}

Our study shows that models trained on small gold datasets face the practical limitations of current \gls{nlp} systems when handling complex tasks. Scaling the amount of high-quality and diverse synthetic documents is a way to address these limitations. It can outperform models trained on real data under certain configurations, thereby validating the approach of generating on-demand data to overcome data scarcity and privacy issues. The evaluation on both \gls{mimic}-III and AlpaCare benchmarks demonstrates the generalizability of our approach across different medical \gls{nlp} task types. These findings facilitate the sharing of high-fidelity synthetic datasets. Furthermore, such datasets may be then annotated using (proprietary) \glspl{llm} or via large-scale manual annotation.

Finally, the proposed solution is more ethical for patients. It focuses on privacy concerns and is motivated by the opening of clinical data for research advancements. While privacy analysis reveals certain vulnerabilities that merit further investigation (addressed in the following chapter), the method provides a practical pathway toward privacy-preserving medical \gls{nlp} development.

\section{Limitations}

While we have expanded our evaluation to include conversational medical tasks through the AlpaCare benchmark, further testing on tasks requiring complex multi-hop reasoning and deep domain-specific knowledge could provide additional insights into the applicability and robustness of our method. The current evaluation demonstrates effectiveness across multiple task types, but more specialized clinical reasoning tasks remain unexplored.

The economical cost for generating large synthetic datasets may also be an issue (see \ref{sec:carbon}) for some healthcare providers, even if it occurs in public environments. Investigating the efficacy of smaller generation models could make this technology more accessible, especially for hospitals or clinics with limited budgets.

Our exploration of alternative evaluation mechanisms—including medical domain filtering and \gls{llm}-as-judge approaches—showed that while these sophisticated techniques offer theoretical advantages, their practical benefits remain moderate in our current pipeline. This suggests that either our bootstrap phase needs strengthening, or domain-specific evaluation criteria tailored to medical synthetic data generation require further investigation.

We are exploring alternative reinforcement learning techniques, such as \gls{kto} (Ethayarajh, 2024), to exploit all the generated data rather than only selecting example pairs filtered by percentile with \gls{dpo}. We are also considering simpler \gls{rl} methods like \gls{orpo} \cite{hong2024orpo} and \gls{simpo} \cite{meng2024simpo}.

Finally, by design, Personal Identifiable Information are absent from our synthetic documents, but privacy analysis reveals vulnerabilities related to re-identification risks that merit dedicated investigation. These privacy considerations are thoroughly examined in the following chapter.
