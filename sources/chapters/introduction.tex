\section{Introduction}
Electronic Health Records (EHR) contain patient and healthcare staff interactions. Professionals record their impressions, observations, and various medical procedures performed. These notes remain fairly expressive and free to save healthcare personnel time and allow for describing unusual situations \citep{rosenbloom_data_2011,wu_survey_2022}. Natural Language Processing (NLP) techniques come here, as they speed up the decision processes~\cite{zhou_datasiftertext_2022,wu_survey_2022}.
In recent years, Proprietary Online Large Language Models (LLMs) such as ChatGPT have shown impressive results using zero or few-shot techniques in analyzing these notes~\cite{agrawal_large_2022, meoni_large_2023, hu_improving_2024}.
However, clinical NLP faces challenges that arise from the sensitive, confidential, and specialized nature of its data—sending such patient information through an external API raises numerous legal issues and is often impossible. Hospitals or third parties providing NLP-based medical devices (i.e., directly impacting patient care) must maintain control over their NLP systems to ensure patient safety. Therefore, the customization of open LLMs and their execution in a secure but computationally constrained environment is an important issue.

However, specific training datasets are necessary to develop a model with clinical skills to address these challenges. To create such a dataset, obtaining real clinical data remains complicated and requires anonymization, which is time-consuming, expensive, and legally constrained. This also hinders the use of online models to annotate real data.
Alternatively, we propose to create synthetic clinical notes that look like real data but do not include patient information~\cite{melamud_towards_2019, ive_generation_2020}. This approach has several benefits: it reduces the need for human input, complies with regulations, and is suitable for annotation with external models to train local models. The local models and datasets can be shared with the community without leaking confidential information. These local models are also small enough to be hosted inside the hospital's infrastructure.

Considering these issues, we implement a novel method for generating synthetic documents, enforcing privacy preservation by design, using only a tiny seed set of pseudo-anonymised data. As a proof of concept, our key contributions include:
\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Privacy-safe Document Generation guided by Clinical Keywords and Reward Mechanism:} We present an methodology that leverages a minimal set of manually pseudo-anonymized data to train fine-tuned generative models. This process is enhanced by enriching prompts with privacy-safe keywords, in our case extracted using QuickUMLS \citep{soldaini_quickumls_2016}, as illustrated in Figures~\ref{sec:experiment}, \ref{fig:workflow}. Furthermore, we improve the quality of the synthetic documents thanks to an iterative refinement process that employs a private scorer to compare real and synthetic documents. This scorer returns only floats as safe scores to the public side, ensuring privacy while enabling continuous improvement of the synthetic document quality.
\item \textbf{Proof of Concept using \mimic:} Because evaluating our methods on real private documents is almost impossible, we utilize the MIMIC-III dataset as a proxy to simulate a private healthcare environment, demonstrating our method's potential in a controlled setting. This proof of concept illustrates how our methodology could be applied in real-world hospital scenarios without compromising patient data.
\item \textbf{Evaluation on downstream tasks using \mimic:} To validate our approach, in particular the quality of the synthetic documents and the pertinence of the scoring, we evaluate the generated data using two tasks: Multilabel Classification based on ICD-9 Codes (ICD-MC) and Synthetic Named Entity Recognition (NER). For ICD-MC, based on the codes proposed by \citet{mullenbach_explainable_2018} and \mimic manual annotations, we have modified this task, as described in Section~\ref{sec:experiment}, to compare the performance of the model trained with real data against the model trained with synthetic data.
The NER task is conducted on annotations returned by GPT-4 on both our synthetic and real data. This allows us to compare the performance of models trained on these datasets. 
\end{itemize}

\section{Background}

The clinical notes contain all the patient and healthcare staff interactions. The health workers indicate their impressions, observations and the various medical acts performed. Despite the widespread adoption of electronic health record systems (EHR), the notes should be kept in an expressive and accessible format to save time for and report unusual situations \citep{Rosenbloom2011DataDocumentation}.

According to a study by \citet{Escudie2017ADisease}, some phenotypic treats of patients are exclusively present in free text. As these documents are unstructured and plenty, they need to be processed with advanced and automatic linguistic methods to be exploitable. This is why NLP research has intensified in recent years \citep{Wu2020DeepReview} and increasingly impacts clinical research \citep{Neveol2018ClinicalChallenges}.

However, the Clinical NLP community faces several challenges due to domain specificities. 
(1) The clinical reports are not freely available due to confidential issues, which makes it difficult to constitute a large corpus and causes some experimentation reproducibility issues \citep{Wu2020DeepReview,Chapman2011OvercomingSolutions}; (2) The clinical reports include a large panel of processes like outpatient visits, inpatient admissions and discharges, procedures, protocols and testing results. A large linguistic variability combines the general and specific language domains, including ambiguity, complex and wide terminology, and heavy use of abbreviations. 
The community's challenges are technical, organizational, and social \citep{Roberts2017LanguageRecord}.

Deep learning research has focused on architecture modelling and shown significant benefits for pretrained large-scale language models. Some few-shots and zero-shot approaches are emerging for extraction and classification tasks in the general domain. 
In this context, prompt and verbalization prediction techniques outperform classical classification with the same amount of examples on some NLP tasks \citep{LeScao2022HowWorth}. Recent study experiments prompt methods in the healthcare domain \citep{Agrawal2022LargeExtractors}; the results are promising and show the same trend as the general domain. However, the limitation of this study is that the datasets used are quite small and based on the CASI and MIMIC-III datasets which can cause representation biases. Above all, the method has not been evaluated in several hospital systems due to confidentiality restrictions and has been limited to the English language. 

To conclude, the result is still mitigated despite the recent advances in NLP.
The clinical domain suffers from the nature of its data, which is both confidential and linguistically specific. The development of new model architectures should not be the major issue; the community should overcome the problem with data in healthcare to get closer to a data-centric machine learning paradigm \citep{Fries2022DatasetModeling}.
