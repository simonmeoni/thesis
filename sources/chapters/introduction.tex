\section{Context: Natural Language Processing for Medical Documents}

The past two decades have witnessed a profound transformation in healthcare documentation, driven by the widespread adoption of Electronic Health Records (\gls{ehr}). These digital repositories now serve as the primary medium for capturing patient information, encompassing diagnoses, medications, treatment plans, laboratory results, imaging reports, and clinical narratives~\citep{shickel2018deep, wu2020deep}. The volume of healthcare data has grown at an unprecedented rate, driven by the digitization of medical records and the proliferation of diagnostic technologies. This exponential growth presents both opportunities and challenges for healthcare systems seeking to leverage their data assets for improved patient care and clinical research.

A critical characteristic of \gls{ehr} data is its heterogeneous nature, comprising both structured elements—such as coded diagnoses, vital signs, and laboratory values—and unstructured free-text narratives written by healthcare providers. Research consistently indicates that approximately 80\% of clinical data exists in unstructured format~\citep{kong2019managing, wu2022neural}, including physician notes, discharge summaries, radiology reports, and nursing assessments. This free-text component captures nuanced clinical information that structured data fields cannot adequately represent: the subtleties of symptom presentation, the reasoning behind clinical decisions, social determinants of health, and the evolution of patient conditions over time~\citep{wang2018clinical, dalianis2018clinical}.

The richness of clinical narratives makes them invaluable for understanding patient trajectories, yet their unstructured nature renders them largely inaccessible to traditional computational analysis. As~\citet{yang2022large} note, clinical notes provide a \textit{glimpse into the physician's brain}, documenting not only what was observed but also the clinical reasoning process. However, without appropriate processing tools, this wealth of information remains effectively locked away, unable to contribute to primary uses such as clinical decision support, quality improvement initiatives, or biomedical research~\citep{wu2022neural, yadav2018mining}.

Natural Language Processing (\gls{nlp}) has emerged as the key technology for unlocking the potential of clinical text~\citep{wang2018clinical, yadav2018mining}. By enabling computational systems to parse, interpret, and extract meaningful information from human language, \gls{nlp} bridges the gap between the expressiveness of clinical narratives and the structured data requirements of downstream applications. The field has undergone a remarkable evolution, progressing from early rule-based systems relying on handcrafted patterns and medical vocabularies to sophisticated machine learning approaches, and more recently to transformer-based language models that have achieved unprecedented performance on a wide range of clinical \gls{nlp} tasks~\citep{kalyan2022ammu, wu2022neural, sahoo2024llm}.

Clinical \gls{nlp} encompasses a diverse array of tasks that collectively enable the transformation of free text into actionable knowledge. Traditional information extraction tasks include Named Entity Recognition (\gls{ner}), which identifies and categorizes clinically relevant mentions—diseases, medications, procedures, anatomical sites—within running text~\citep{wang2019clinical, nasar2021named}. Relation extraction (RE) determines the semantic connections between identified entities, such as linking a drug to its dosage or a symptom to its anatomical location~\citep{frailenavarroClinicalNamedEntity2023}. Temporal reasoning situates clinical events within a timeline, enabling the reconstruction of patient histories~\citep{bethard2017semeval}. Assertion detection and negation handling distinguish between conditions that are present, absent, hypothetical, or attributed to family members~\citep{chapman2013extending}. Text classification supports tasks ranging from document triage to phenotype identification~\citep{yim2016natural}. Together, these capabilities form the foundation for applications such as automated coding, pharmacovigilance, and cohort identification for clinical trials~\citep{kreimeyer2017natural, wang2018clinical}.

The methodological landscape of clinical \gls{nlp} has evolved through successive paradigms. Early systems relied on rule-based approaches, leveraging handcrafted patterns, regular expressions, and medical ontologies such as \gls{umls} and \gls{snomed}-CT to identify clinical concepts~\citep{friedman2004natural}. While interpretable and precise for well-defined patterns, these systems required extensive manual engineering and struggled with the variability inherent in clinical language. The introduction of machine learning brought statistical methods---including Conditional Random Fields (\glspl{crf}) and Support Vector Machines---that could learn patterns from annotated corpora, improving robustness to linguistic variation~\citep{jiang2011systematic}. The deep learning revolution, beginning around 2015, saw recurrent neural networks and \gls{lstm}-\gls{crf} architectures achieve state-of-the-art performance on sequence labeling tasks by learning distributed representations of clinical text~\citep{wu2018deep, si2019enhancing}. However, the most transformative shift came with the introduction of transformer-based pre-trained language models. \gls{bert}~\citep{devlin2019bert} and its biomedical and clinical variants---BioBERT~\citep{lee2020biobert}, ClinicalBERT~\citep{huang2019clinicalbert}, and PubMedBERT~\citep{gu2021domain}---demonstrated that self-supervised pre-training on large corpora yields rich contextual representations that transfer effectively to downstream tasks with minimal task-specific data. Scaling these approaches, GatorTron trained on over 90 billion words of text established that larger models and more diverse clinical training data yield consistent improvements across benchmarks~\citep{yang2022large}.

The applications of clinical \gls{nlp} extend across the entire healthcare continuum. In hospital settings, \gls{nlp}-powered systems can process millions of clinical documents to extract structured information supporting epidemiological surveillance, quality measurement, and operational analytics~\citep{bean2023hospital}. For clinical research, automated phenotyping algorithms combining \gls{nlp} with rule-based logic enable the identification of patient cohorts at scales impossible through manual chart review~\citep{kirby2016phekb}. In pharmaceutical development, text mining of both clinical records and biomedical literature accelerates drug discovery, pharmacovigilance, and the identification of drug-drug interactions~\citep{harpaz2014text}. Clinical decision support systems increasingly incorporate \gls{nlp} components to provide real-time alerts, suggest diagnoses, or recommend evidence-based interventions based on the information contained in clinical notes~\citep{sutton2020overview}.

The emergence of Large Language Models (\glspl{llm}) has fundamentally expanded the scope of clinical \gls{nlp} beyond traditional information extraction toward natural language generation and complex reasoning tasks~\citep{singhal2023large, thirunavukarasu2023large}. Clinical text summarization has become a major focus, with \glspl{llm} demonstrating the ability to condense lengthy patient records, radiology reports, and doctor-patient dialogues into concise, actionable summaries. Recent studies have shown that adapted \glspl{llm} can produce summaries that physicians rate as equivalent or superior to those written by medical experts in terms of completeness, correctness, and conciseness~\citep{vanveen2024adapted}. The automatic generation of discharge summaries represents a particularly impactful application, with several systems demonstrating significant reductions in documentation time while maintaining clinical accuracy. These advances directly address the documentation burden that contributes to physician burnout.

Beyond summarization, \glspl{llm} enable entirely new paradigms for clinical decision support. Medical question answering systems can now engage in multi-turn dialogues, providing contextually appropriate responses to complex clinical queries~\citep{singhal2023large}. Diagnostic reasoning represents another frontier, with studies demonstrating that \glspl{llm} can match or exceed physician performance on clinical reasoning assessments, though they also exhibit higher rates of incorrect reasoning, underscoring the need for human oversight~\citep{cabral2024clinical}. \glspl{llm} have also shown promise for patient-facing applications, including the translation of clinical documentation into patient-friendly language, medication counseling, and preliminary symptom assessment. Crucially, the zero-shot and few-shot learning capabilities of modern \glspl{llm}---first demonstrated at scale by GPT-3~\citep{brown2020language}---reduce dependence on large annotated datasets, potentially democratizing access to clinical \gls{nlp} tools across institutions and languages that lack extensive training resources~\citep{hu2024improving}.

Despite these advances, persistent challenges remain. The linguistic characteristics of clinical text—including pervasive abbreviations, telegraphic style, misspellings, and domain-specific jargon—differ substantially from the general-domain text on which most \gls{nlp} models are trained~\citep{dalianis2018clinical}. The sensitive nature of health information imposes strict requirements for data governance, limiting access to training data and constraining the development of robust, generalizable models. Variability across institutions in documentation practices, \gls{ehr} systems, and clinical workflows means that models trained in one setting may not transfer effectively to another~\citep{wu2022neural}. Furthermore, the high-stakes nature of healthcare demands levels of accuracy, interpretability, and reliability that current systems struggle to consistently achieve~\citep{wu2022neural}. These challenges motivate continued research into methods that can deliver the promise of clinical \gls{nlp} while respecting the unique constraints of the healthcare domain.

\section{Problem Statement: Data and Privacy, a Fundamental Tension}

The remarkable progress of clinical \gls{nlp} systems has been driven by a seemingly simple principle: more data yields better models. Deep learning architectures, and particularly large language models, are fundamentally data-hungry, with performance improvements following predictable scaling laws that link model capability to training corpus size. GatorTron's success with 90 billion words of clinical text exemplifies this paradigm---larger models trained on more diverse clinical data consistently outperform their smaller counterparts~\citep{yang2022large}. More recently, Med-PaLM~2 achieved 86.5\% accuracy on the MedQA benchmark through a combination of scale (building on the 540-billion parameter PaLM model) and medical domain fine-tuning, with physician evaluators preferring its answers to those of human doctors on eight of nine clinical axes~\citep{singhal2023medpalm2}. These advances underscore a consistent finding: performance gains in medical \gls{ai} correlate strongly with access to large-scale, high-quality clinical data.

However, recent research has begun to nuance this purely volumetric paradigm.
Studies on data curation demonstrate that quality can partially compensate for quantity: the Phi-1 model, trained on approximately 7 billion tokens of carefully selected ``textbook-quality'' data, outperformed models trained on datasets orders of magnitude larger~\citep{gunasekar2023textbooks}, while the LIMA study showed that just 1,000 curated examples could produce alignment results rivaling models fine-tuned on far more data~\citep{zhou2023lima}.
In the clinical domain, this principle is particularly salient---models pre-trained on focused, high-quality clinical text consistently outperform those trained on vastly larger general-domain corpora~\citep{gu2021domain}.
This quality-over-quantity dynamic has implications for synthetic data generation: the challenge becomes not merely generating documents at scale, but ensuring they meet the quality standards that enable effective learning.

Yet in the medical domain, the appetite for data---whether prioritizing volume or quality---collides directly with the imperative to protect patient privacy, creating a fundamental tension that shapes and constrains the development of clinical \gls{nlp} systems.

Health information occupies a privileged position in data protection frameworks worldwide, recognized as among the most sensitive categories of personal data. The European Union's General Data Protection Regulation (\gls{gdpr}) classifies health data as a ``special category'' under Article 9, subject to a general prohibition on processing with only narrow exceptions for purposes such as medical diagnosis, healthcare provision, or public health research---and even these require explicit legal bases and appropriate safeguards~\citep{gdpr2016}. In the United States, the Health Insurance Portability and Accountability Act (\gls{hipaa}) Privacy Rule establishes national standards protecting individually identifiable health information, restricting its use and disclosure while granting patients rights over their medical records~\citep{hipaa1996}. Similar frameworks exist across jurisdictions: France's Loi Informatique et Libertés, as amended to align with \gls{gdpr}, maintains additional protections for health data processing~\citep{cnil2018loi}, while the UK's Data Protection Act 2018 preserves \gls{gdpr}-equivalent safeguards post-Brexit with specific provisions for health research~\citep{ukdpa2018}. Sector-specific regulations in healthcare systems globally reinforce these protections. These frameworks share a common recognition that health data merits heightened protection due to the potential for discrimination, stigmatization, and personal harm that could follow from unauthorized disclosure.

Traditional approaches to enabling data sharing while protecting privacy have centered on de-identification: the removal or transformation of identifying information to render records no longer attributable to specific individuals. \gls{hipaa} defines two pathways for de-identification: the Safe Harbor method, which requires removal of 18 specific identifier types including names, geographic subdivisions smaller than a state, and dates more specific than year; and the Expert Determination method, which requires a qualified statistician to certify that the risk of re-identification is ``very small''~\citep{hipaa1996}. Similar principles underpin \gls{gdpr}'s concept of anonymization, under which properly anonymized data falls outside the regulation's scope entirely.

However, the reliability of de-identification has been called into question by research demonstrating successful re-identification attacks. A seminal study showed that 87\% of the U.S. population could be uniquely identified by the combination of gender, date of birth, and five-digit ZIP code---information routinely retained in ``de-identified'' datasets~\citep{sweeney2002}. Subsequent work has extended these findings, demonstrating that even datasets de-identified to the \gls{hipaa} Safe Harbor standard remain vulnerable when combined with external information sources such as news reports or public records~\citep{elEmam2011}. The re-identification risk is particularly acute for clinical text, where removing explicit identifiers does not eliminate the narrative details---rare conditions, unusual circumstances, distinctive timelines---that can render individuals uniquely identifiable within their communities.

A second limitation of de-identification concerns the trade-off between privacy protection and data utility. Aggressive de-identification that removes or generalizes potentially identifying information inevitably degrades the clinical and linguistic richness that makes the data valuable for \gls{nlp} research. Dates become years, locations become regions, and specific clinical details may be suppressed entirely. For tasks that depend on temporal reasoning, geographic variation in care patterns, or the precise semantics of clinical narratives, heavily de-identified data may no longer support the intended analyses. This creates a paradox: the more thoroughly data is protected, the less useful it becomes for training and evaluating \gls{nlp} systems that must ultimately operate on fully detailed clinical text.

These constraints have direct consequences for clinical \gls{nlp} research. Unlike domains where training data can be scraped from the open web or crowdsourced from willing participants, clinical text resides behind institutional firewalls, governed by ethics committees, data use agreements, and legal constraints. Researchers typically cannot access clinical notes from hospitals where they are not employed---a limitation I have experienced firsthand during this thesis work; even within institutions, data access requires navigating complex governance structures. Cross-institutional collaborations face additional hurdles, as sharing identifiable data across organizational boundaries often requires patient consent or formal data sharing agreements that are slow to negotiate and limited in scope. The result is a fragmented and paradoxical landscape. On one hand, the few publicly available clinical datasets---notably \gls{mimic} and the i2b2/n2c2 challenge corpora---become de facto standards on which most published models are trained and evaluated, despite representing narrow slices of clinical practice (predominantly intensive care units at a single US institution, in the case of \gls{mimic}). Models optimized for these benchmarks may not generalize to the diversity of documentation styles, patient populations, and clinical workflows encountered in real-world settings. On the other hand, institutions that develop models on their own proprietary data often cannot share these models or the datasets underlying them.
This restriction extends to the models themselves because neural networks can memorize and inadvertently expose their training data: membership inference attacks can determine whether specific patient records were used during training~\citep{shokri2017membership}, while training data extraction attacks have demonstrated the ability to recover verbatim training examples from language models~\citep{carlini2021extracting}.
These vulnerabilities mean that sharing a model trained on sensitive clinical text creates privacy risks comparable to sharing the underlying data, effectively siloing practical clinical \gls{nlp} tools within individual healthcare systems. This disconnect between academic benchmarks and clinical reality means that published state-of-the-art results may not translate to operational performance, while genuinely useful institutional tools never enter the scientific literature.

These limitations of traditional approaches have motivated growing interest in synthetic data as an alternative paradigm for enabling clinical \gls{nlp} research while preserving privacy. Synthetic clinical documents are artificially generated texts that mimic the statistical and linguistic properties of real clinical notes without corresponding to any actual patient. When properly generated, synthetic data preserves the semantic richness and domain-specific characteristics that make clinical text valuable for \gls{nlp} development while severing the link to identifiable individuals that triggers privacy protections.

The potential of synthetic data has been amplified by advances in generative language modeling. Modern \glspl{llm} have demonstrated remarkable capability in producing fluent, contextually appropriate text across domains, including medicine. Systems like Synthea pioneered rule-based generation of complete synthetic patient records, while more recent work has leveraged neural language models to generate clinical notes that capture the stylistic and terminological characteristics of authentic documentation~\citep{walonoski2018synthea}. Studies have shown that augmenting real clinical datasets with synthetic examples can improve downstream \gls{nlp} performance, particularly for minority classes and rare conditions where authentic training data is scarce~\citep{tang2023synthetic_clinical_llm, kumichev2024medsyn}. Synthetic data generation may thus address not only privacy constraints but also the class imbalance and data scarcity challenges endemic to clinical \gls{nlp}.

Yet the promise of synthetic data comes with significant caveats. Unlike general-domain text generation, where plausibility is the primary criterion, clinical synthetic data must satisfy additional requirements of medical coherence, terminological accuracy, and representativeness of actual clinical practice. Generating text that reads like clinical notes is insufficient; the generated content must reflect realistic patient presentations, appropriate clinical reasoning, and accurate relationships between symptoms, diagnoses, and treatments. Furthermore, the very capability that makes \glspl{llm} effective generators---their training on vast corpora that may include clinical text---raises questions about whether synthetic outputs might inadvertently leak information about the training data, thereby undermining privacy guarantees.

These considerations motivate the concept of \textit{facsimile documents}, where synthetic clinical texts are not generated from scratch but rather from medical keywords and concepts extracted from real patient records.
By grounding generation in the clinical vocabulary and narrative structure of authentic documents while discarding identifying content, facsimile documents occupy a middle ground between purely fictional text and direct data sharing.
This thesis investigates such an approach and its implications across generation quality, downstream utility, and residual privacy risks.

The quality requirements for clinical \gls{nlp} training data are particularly demanding. Unlike general-domain \gls{nlp}, where models can learn from billions of web-scraped tokens of variable quality, clinical \gls{nlp} benefits disproportionately from high-quality, domain-specific data. The specialized vocabulary, abbreviated writing style, and domain-specific reasoning patterns of clinical text require training corpora that accurately represent these characteristics. Models pre-trained on general text and fine-tuned on clinical data consistently underperform those trained on clinical corpora from the outset~\citep{gu2021domain}. This emphasis on data quality over mere quantity places additional demands on synthetic data generation: synthetic clinical text must not only be privacy-preserving and plentiful but also linguistically and medically authentic.
\section{Research Questions}

The preceding analysis reveals a fundamental challenge: clinical \gls{nlp} requires access to large-scale, high-quality training data, yet privacy regulations and institutional constraints severely limit such access. Synthetic data generation offers a promising path forward, but its effectiveness for clinical \gls{nlp} applications remains incompletely understood. This thesis investigates the potential of synthetic medical document generation to address the data scarcity problem in clinical \gls{nlp}, organized around three interconnected research questions.

\textbf{RQ1: How can large language models be leveraged to generate synthetic clinical documents that are both linguistically realistic and medically coherent?}

The first research question addresses the fundamental challenge of generation quality. Clinical text possesses distinctive characteristics---specialized terminology, abbreviated writing conventions, implicit domain knowledge, and complex reasoning patterns---that distinguish it from general-domain text. Generating synthetic documents that faithfully reproduce these characteristics requires understanding what makes clinical text ``authentic'' and developing generation strategies that preserve these properties. This question encompasses the selection and adaptation of language models for clinical text generation, the design of prompting or fine-tuning strategies that encode medical knowledge, and the development of evaluation frameworks capable of assessing both linguistic quality and clinical validity.

\textbf{RQ2: To what extent can synthetic clinical documents serve as effective training data for downstream \gls{nlp} tasks, and under what conditions do they provide benefit over real data alone?}

The second research question concerns the utility of synthetic data for practical clinical \gls{nlp} applications. Even if synthetic documents appear realistic, they must demonstrate functional equivalence to real clinical text when used for training \gls{nlp} models.
Conversely, documents that lack surface-level realism may nevertheless prove effective for training if they capture the underlying linguistic patterns and label distributions that models need to learn. This question investigates whether models trained on synthetic data---either exclusively or in combination with limited real data---can achieve performance comparable to models trained on larger real datasets. It also explores the conditions under which synthetic data augmentation provides the greatest benefit: for rare conditions, minority classes, or specific document types where real training examples are scarce.

\textbf{RQ3: Can large language models serve as effective annotators for clinical text, and how does \gls{llm}-based annotation compare to traditional expert annotation for training downstream \gls{nlp} models?}

The third research question extends the investigation of \glspl{llm} beyond text generation to the complementary challenge of data annotation. Supervised clinical \gls{nlp} systems require labeled training data, yet expert annotation of clinical text is expensive, time-consuming, and difficult to scale across languages and institutions. Recent advances in \gls{llm} capabilities suggest they might serve as automated annotators, potentially democratizing access to labeled clinical data. This question investigates the quality of \gls{llm}-generated annotations compared to human experts, explores strategies for improving annotation accuracy through prompting or domain adaptation, and examines whether models trained on \gls{llm}-annotated data can achieve performance comparable to those trained on expert-annotated corpora.

\textbf{RQ4: What are the privacy implications of synthetic clinical text generation, and how can generation approaches be designed to minimize re-identification risks while preserving data utility?}

The fourth research question addresses the privacy guarantees that motivate interest in synthetic data. If synthetic documents inadvertently memorize and reproduce identifiable information from training corpora, they may fail to deliver the privacy benefits that justify their use. This question examines the extent to which current generation approaches risk leaking sensitive information, explores techniques for quantifying and mitigating such risks, and investigates the trade-offs between privacy protection and the preservation of clinically relevant details that make synthetic data useful for \gls{nlp} development.
\section{Contributions}

This thesis makes several contributions to clinical \gls{nlp}, spanning synthetic data generation, privacy-preserving evaluation, and knowledge distillation for multilingual clinical entity extraction. These contributions are grounded in peer-reviewed publications and address the research questions outlined above.

\paragraph{A keyword-based pipeline for synthetic clinical document generation.} We introduce a privacy-sensitive approach to generating synthetic clinical documents that avoids direct identification risks by design. Rather than conditioning generation on complete patient records, our method extracts semantically relevant medical keywords from real documents---diagnostic codes, symptoms, procedures---while discarding identifying information. These keywords serve as controlled inputs for large language model generation, ensuring that the resulting documents maintain clinical relevance without copying sensitive patient details. The approach incorporates a reward-based mechanism that iteratively refines generation quality through semantic similarity scoring against real clinical reports, followed by an alignment step that steers the model toward higher-quality outputs. This pipeline addresses RQ1 by demonstrating that clinically coherent synthetic documents can be generated from privacy-preserving seed information. This work was published at the Clinical \gls{nlp} Workshop~\citep{meoni2024generating, meoni2025synthetic}.

\paragraph{Evaluation of synthetic data utility for downstream clinical \gls{nlp} tasks.} We conduct systematic experiments evaluating the utility of generated synthetic documents for training clinical \gls{nlp} models. Our evaluation spans multiple tasks including multi-label classification (\gls{icd} code prediction) and named entity recognition, comparing models trained on synthetic data, real data, and their combinations. The experiments demonstrate conditions under which synthetic data augmentation yields performance improvements, particularly for minority classes and rare clinical concepts where authentic training examples are scarce. This contribution addresses RQ2 by establishing empirical evidence for the functional equivalence of synthetic and real clinical data under specific conditions~\citep{meoni2025synthetic}.

\paragraph{Knowledge distillation for multilingual clinical entity extraction.} We explore the use of large language models as annotators for clinical named entity recognition, framing this as a knowledge distillation problem where \gls{llm}-generated annotations serve as weak supervision for training smaller, deployable models. Our study provides a systematic analysis of \gls{llm} annotation quality across multiple languages, with particular focus on French clinical text where expert-annotated resources are scarce. We examine prompting strategies that improve annotation accuracy and evaluate whether models trained on \gls{llm}-generated silver-standard annotations can achieve performance competitive with those trained on expert-labeled gold-standard data. The results demonstrate that \gls{llm}-based annotation offers a viable path toward clinical \gls{nlp} in low-resource language settings, reducing dependence on expensive expert annotation while maintaining acceptable downstream performance. This contribution addresses RQ3 and was published at the BioNLP Workshop~\citep{meoni2023large}.

\paragraph{Privacy analysis and re-identification risk assessment.} While our keyword-based approach mitigates direct identification risks, we conduct a comprehensive analysis of residual privacy vulnerabilities. We observe that semantically-driven generation, though avoiding explicit identifiers, may still enable re-identification through combinations of rare clinical attributes. To quantify these risks, we implement multiple threat models and re-identification attacks against synthetic corpora, evaluating both membership inference and attribute linkage scenarios. We further investigate rephrasing-based mitigation strategies that introduce controlled variation while preserving clinical semantics, systematically characterizing the trade-off between privacy protection and data utility. This contribution addresses RQ4 by providing both empirical risk quantification and practical mitigation approaches.

\section{Thesis Outline}
This thesis is organized into seven chapters, progressing from foundational concepts through methodological contributions to synthesis and conclusions.

\textbf{Chapter~\ref{chap:related}} presents the related work that situates this thesis within the broader landscape of clinical \gls{nlp} research. It begins with an overview of available resources for clinical \gls{nlp}, then examines data augmentation techniques spanning pre-\gls{llm} approaches, \gls{llm}-based methods, and generation with feedback mechanisms. The chapter also covers alignment and self-learning strategies, privacy-preserving approaches in sensitive domains---including anonymization, pseudonymization, and differential privacy---and concludes with evaluation criteria for synthetic data encompassing utility, semantic coherence, and privacy risk assessment.

\textbf{Chapter~\ref{chap:synthetic}} addresses RQ1 and RQ2 by introducing a synthetic document generation pipeline for medical tasks. The chapter presents a reward-based generation mechanism that combines keyword injection with multi-step generation, scoring, and alignment to produce clinically coherent synthetic documents. Experiments on the AlpaCare benchmark evaluate the generated data on downstream tasks including multi-label classification and named entity recognition, demonstrating the conditions under which synthetic data augmentation improves model performance.

\textbf{Chapter~\ref{chap:french-application}} extends the generation pipeline to a French clinical setting, addressing the practical challenges of applying our approach beyond English and the \gls{mimic} dataset. The chapter adapts the scoring mechanism, evaluates cross-lingual transfer, and demonstrates that the methodology generalizes to a different language and institutional context.

\textbf{Chapter~\ref{chap:distillation}} addresses RQ3 through an investigation of knowledge distillation with large language models for multilingual clinical entity extraction. The chapter explores \gls{llm}-based annotation as weak supervision, examining prompting strategies for generating silver-standard annotations. Experiments evaluate both monolingual and multilingual settings, with detailed analysis of French clinical entity extraction demonstrating the potential of \gls{llm}-generated annotations to reduce dependence on expert-labeled data.

\textbf{Chapter~\ref{chap:privacy}} addresses RQ4 through a comprehensive privacy analysis of synthetic clinical documents. The chapter formalizes threat models for re-identification attacks, implements linkage and discrimination attack scenarios using both lexical and semantic similarity metrics, and quantifies privacy vulnerabilities in baseline generation. It then proposes and evaluates mitigation strategies including rephrasing and keyword perturbation, systematically analyzing the trade-offs between privacy protection and downstream task utility.


\textbf{Chapter~\ref{chap:discussion}} synthesizes the contributions across all chapters, providing critical analysis of the proposed approaches including their strengths and limitations. It positions the work with respect to the current state of the art, discusses implications for the medical domain, and outlines research perspectives for both short-term extensions and long-term directions.

\textbf{Chapter~\ref{chap:conclusion}} concludes the thesis by recapitulating the context and problem, summarizing the contributions across synthetic document generation, evaluation pipeline improvements, and knowledge distillation for entity extraction. It provides explicit answers to the research questions and offers perspectives on future developments in privacy-preserving clinical \gls{nlp}.

The thesis includes two appendices: Appendix~A provides detailed results for the weak supervision experiments across languages and models, while Appendix~B presents additional materials for synthetic document generation including prompt templates and example documents.
